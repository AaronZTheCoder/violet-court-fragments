# The Violet Court Fragments

## The Reconstruction

> _When a levee is put back, the work is called reconstruction._
> _When the account of the levee is put back, the work is called reconstruction._
> _The Bureau has never seen a reason to use two words for this._

> _Standing instruction to copyists of the Eighth Rank, Bureau of Harmonious Records_

---

## The Word

Every empire has a method for surviving what it has broken. The Nine Domains have an instrument, and the instrument has a name, and the name is a piece of ordinary vocabulary that no one hears any more.

Reconstruction is what happens after. It is the word a farmer uses for a rebuilt dike and the word a Bureau uses for a rebuilt file, and the two meanings were joined so quietly that no official can point to the year it happened. There was no decree. There was a form, and the form had a field, and the field was labeled Reconstruction, and one clerk in the reign of the Ledger Hand began entering both kinds of work into it because both kinds of work crossed his desk.

That clerk is the most consequential person in the history of the empire and nobody knows his name.

Your players work inside this instrument. They are brush scribes of the Eighth Rank in the Bureau of Harmonious Records. When a province drowns, a document is generated, and the document passes through hands like theirs, and what the empire will remember about the drowning is decided somewhere in that passage. They are not observers of the plot. They are the mechanism of it.

This is the thing to hold on to when you run this game. The Court's error is not cruelty. Grandmother Stone Voice put it better than the Court ever has: the error is abstraction, and every atrocity in the empire has been committed by someone working from a summary. The Reconstruction is the empire's summary machine. It is staffed almost entirely by people who would describe themselves as decent, because it is.

## The Gloss and the Key

The Court does not write plainly. It has not written plainly in one hundred and forty years, and the reason is administrative rather than sinister, which is what makes it worse.

An order that says what it means can be produced in evidence. An order that says what it means can be understood by the clerk who carries it, and a clerk who understands what he carries may decline to carry it, or may sell it, or may weep. So the Court developed the Gloss.

**What the Gloss is.** Every instruction above the sixth rank is written twice. There is a base text, which exists in one copy, in a room in the Spire District that most officials do not believe is real. And there is the glossed copy, which is what everyone else receives. The glossed copy is grammatical, courteous, and complete. It concerns granary rotation, or road maintenance, or the humidity of an archive. It is not code in the sense of a cipher, because a cipher announces that it is a cipher. The Gloss announces nothing. It reads exactly like the tedious document it pretends to be, and most of the time it also genuinely is that document, because the Bureau long ago learned that the safest place to keep a sentence is inside a hundred thousand sentences that resemble it.

**The Key.** The correspondence between the glossed copy and the base text is held in an instrument called the Key. The Key is not a book. It is a set of relations, maintained by a section of the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative whose members are rotated every four years and are not permitted to know each other's assignments. A term that meant one thing under the previous Key means something adjacent and slightly wrong under the current one.

**The Key turns at the Hinge.** Five days at the end of the year belong to no month. They are the empire's spare change, the days nobody is responsible for, and this is when the Key rotates. On the first intercalary day the correspondences shift. Nothing is announced, because announcing it would defeat it. The Bureau simply issues the new season's instructions in the new register and expects the empire to keep up.

**What this costs.** Orders outlive their meaning.

An instruction drafted under one Key and executed under the next does something that no living person intended. There is a term of art for this inside the Bureau. They call it a carry. Most carries are harmless: a granary is inspected twice, a road is surfaced that did not need surfacing. Some are not.

The withdrawal of the Scorch Field residence code in Year 414 was a carry. The instruction had been drafted in the previous register, where the relevant phrase meant suspend pending survey. Under the new register it meant close. Between sixty thousand and one hundred and forty thousand people became legally nonexistent because a correspondence shifted during five days that belonged to no month, and the clerk who executed it did so correctly, and was commended.

Nobody in the Court has ever been punished for a carry. You cannot punish a man for reading an instruction the way the instrument told him to read it. This is not a loophole in the system. It is the system's finest feature, and everyone senior enough to understand it understands it: the Gloss does not merely conceal who gave an order. It dissolves the question of whether anyone did.

**At the table.** Give your players glossed documents. Actual ones. Write out the granary schedule and let them hold it. Somewhere in it is a name, or a date, or a quantity that does not fit the surrounding arithmetic, and the Intellect check is not to read the document, because anyone can read it. The check is to notice that it is a document about something else.

And when they finally decode one, do not let them be certain. That is the whole horror of the Gloss. The best they will ever have is a reading, and a reading is a thing that can be wrong, and if they act on a wrong reading the consequences are real and are theirs.

## The Near View and the Far View

The Bureau of Harmonious Narrative teaches its cadets exactly one thing, and teaches it in the first week, and never needs to teach it again.

**The Near View.** A face. A pair of hands. A soldier carrying stone in water to his chest for eleven hours. A mother, found alive. A child's shoe in the silt, recovered and returned. The Near View is true. This matters enormously and is the reason it works. The Bureau does not fabricate the soldier. The soldier is real, and he did carry stone for eleven hours, and he is owed something the empire will never pay him.

**The Far View.** Why the water rose to that height in the first place. What the flood lakes were before they were fields. Who signed the survey. What the levee cost on paper and what it cost in stone. The Far View is also true. It is simply further away, and it has no face.

The instruction, which appears verbatim in the Bureau's cadet manual and has appeared there for eighty one years, is this: _give them a face and they will not ask for a map._

The Bureau is not lying to the empire. That is what your players will find hardest to accept, and what will corrode them fastest. Every image the Bureau circulates is accurate. It is the arrangement that lies, and an arrangement cannot be caught in a lie, because no individual sentence in it is false.

A player who tries to publish the Far View will discover the second half of the doctrine, which is not written in the manual because it does not need to be. The Far View has no face. Therefore it has no audience. Therefore it does not require suppression. It requires only that nothing be done, by anyone, for eleven days, and then it is old.

## The Six Reconstructions

The instrument has been rewritten five times. Each rewrite was forced by a failure of the version before it, and each was drafted by competent people solving a real problem, and this is the sequence that has brought the empire to where your campaign begins.

A sixth is in draft. It has not been issued.

---

### The First Reconstruction: The One Lamp

**Issued Year 371, under the Ledger Hand.**

**The failure.** In Year 370 the great river left its channel and took the low prefectures. The Court's difficulty was not the water. The Court's difficulty was that two surveyors of the Verdant Path, a woman called Low Weir and a man called Slack Tide, spent the following two years measuring, and published what they measured.

Their finding was simple enough that a farmer could follow it. Less water had come down the river than in the flood of the previous century. The water had stood higher anyway. It had stood higher because the flood lakes that once took the overflow had been drained for fields across sixty years of excellent, well documented, individually reasonable decisions, and because the upper forests had been cut, and because the silt those forests had held was now in the riverbed, raising it, so that in eleven prefectures the river ran above the roofs of the towns beside it.

The flood was weather. The disaster was bookkeeping.

**The method.** The Court held the only lamp. In an empire where word travels at the speed of a horse, the Bureau does not have to argue with an account. It has only to be the account. What the criers said had happened was what had happened, and the Bureau discovered in that year that grief is not dangerous if it is given somewhere to go before it finds a question.

**The instrument.** The Concord Vigil. Within nine days of any calamity, every prefectural shrine in the Nine Domains lights at the same hour, the same rite is spoken, the same names of the brave are read, and the empire weeps together on a schedule. It is genuinely moving. Survivors have described it as the only time they felt the empire knew they existed. It is also, measured by the Bureau's own internal review, the single most effective instrument the Court has ever built, because a population that has finished mourning on the ninth day is not a population that begins asking on the tenth.

**What it cost.** The Silt Papers were not burned. That is the detail that matters. They were filed, correctly, in the Imperial Archive, under hydrology, where they remain. Low Weir was offered a chair at the Academy and took it. Slack Tide refused, kept measuring, and was eventually charged with an unrelated irregularity in his residence papers.

**What your players can find.** The Silt Papers, complete, in the west wing. Nobody is guarding them. Nobody has requested them in thirty years. The Court did not need to destroy the Far View. It only needed to be sure that the Near View arrived first.

### The Second Reconstruction: The Ten Breaths

**Issued Year 410, under the Accord.**

**The failure.** The earth moved in the western prefectures and eighty thousand people died, five thousand of them children, in schools that folded like wet paper while the offices beside them stood. The building codes existed. The inspections had been signed. Somewhere between the allocation and the concrete, in a chain of small men each taking a small percentage, the steel had thinned.

The One Lamp failed within the hour, and it failed for a reason nobody in the Bureau had anticipated: the Iron Calculation had spent the previous decade stringing Qi lantern relays between the prefectures so that grain prices could be settled the same day. The relays did not care what they carried. On the afternoon of the earthquake, word of it reached the capital along the grain lines roughly ten breaths before it reached the Court through the Court's own channels.

Ten breaths. That is the whole of it. The Bureau's monopoly did not end because of a philosophy or a rebellion. It ended because a machine built to price barley was faster than a horse.

**The method.** Stop trying to be first. Be loudest, and be helpful, and be everywhere.

The Second Reconstruction is the moment the Court stopped trying to own the account and began trying to own the arrangement of accounts. Every rescue story the people themselves told was collected, copied, and pushed outward by the Bureau's own relays with the Bureau's own seal on it. The Court became the largest distributor of other people's true stories in the history of the Nine Domains, and it did this sincerely, and the stories were good, and people cried at them.

**The instrument.** Two things, issued the same week.

The first was the harvest: a standing section of the Bureau whose entire function is to read what ordinary people are saying and elevate the fraction of it that flatters. Not to write. To choose.

The second was a charge that had not previously existed in imperial law, because it had not previously needed to: **false weaving**. To weave is to originate an untrue account. To carry is to pass one along. The oath now recited by every registered guild, shrine, and teahouse in the empire is three phrases long and every child in the Nine Domains can say it: _weave nothing, carry nothing, credit nothing._

Note the third clause. The first two govern what you do. The third governs what you believe, and it is the only one that cannot be enforced, and it is the one the Bureau considers its masterpiece, because a person who has sworn not to credit things will do the Bureau's work inside his own head for free.

**What it cost.** A citizen archivist began compiling the names of the dead children, because the Court would not. He was detained. The charge was disturbing public order, which is what the charge was in Year 410, because false weaving had been issued eleven days earlier and the prefectural office had not yet been trained in it.

The list, incomplete, exists in three copies. Shadow has one.

**What your players can find.** The training memorandum that circulated to prefectural offices between the earthquake and the issuance. It is four pages. It is entirely about procedure. On the third page it explains, in the flattest possible language, how to classify an account that is true but unhelpful.

### The Third Reconstruction: The Lent Seal

**Issued Year 417, under the Accord.**

**The failure.** Two Qi rail carriages collided on an elevated line and fell. Before the search for survivors was complete, before the families had arrived, the Bureau of Internal Harmony ordered the wreckage buried in a pit beside the track. When asked why, the spokesman said the burial was necessary for the rescue work to proceed. A survivor was pulled from the wreckage two hours after the burial began.

The Second Reconstruction could not process this, and the reason is structural. The Ten Breaths works by making the Court the largest voice in a chorus of true rescue stories. But the carriages were the Court's own line, built by the Court's own school, praised in the Court's own bulletins for four years. There was no rescuer to be. The Court was the thing that had happened.

The phrase entered the language within a month. In the Nine Domains, when an official explains something in a way that insults the listener's intelligence, people say: _he is burying the carriage._ They say it quietly. But they say it.

**The method.** Stop cleaning up after speech. Make speech someone else's liability.

**The instrument.** The Lent Seal. Every house of assembly in the empire, every teahouse above a certain size, every guild hall, scriptorium, shrine, and market company, must hold a seal issued by the Bureau of Internal Harmony. The seal is a privilege. It is also a debt. The holder of the seal answers personally for every account spoken or copied beneath his roof, and the answering is not theoretical, and the holder knows it.

Alongside it, the Counting Rule: an account judged false and carried past five hundred hands is no longer a local matter. Five hundred. The number is not large. Any teahouse keeper in the Outer Market can do the arithmetic on the back of a receipt, and every one of them has.

**What it cost.** The Court stopped needing to censor. That is what the Third Reconstruction actually achieved and it is why it has never been replaced, only extended. There are perhaps four hundred officials in the Bureau of Internal Harmony who read anything at all. There are, by the Bureau's own most recent estimate, one hundred and ninety thousand people in the Nine Domains employed full time in reading and striking, and the Court pays almost none of them.

**What your players can find.** A teahouse keeper who has been holding a seal for nine years and has struck one thousand four hundred accounts and can recite from memory the seven he regrets. He is not a villain. He has three children. Put him at the table with them.

### The Fourth Reconstruction: The Turned Mirror

**Issued Year 446, under the Iron Brush.**

**The failure.** The Second Breath. A plague in a lake city of the central plain, large enough that no one had ever counted it properly. The first physicians to describe it were summoned by the local Commission office and made to sign statements admitting they had spread rumors. One of them died of the disease he had been disciplined for reporting.

Then the Court moved, and moved with a totality no other power on earth could have matched, and drove the disease to almost nothing while the rest of the world buried its dead in trenches. For two years the empire was the safest place in the world and its citizens knew it and were proud.

Then it went on. And on. Sealed courtyards. Grain that did not arrive. A fire in a tenement where the stair doors had been barred from outside.

Every previous Reconstruction assumed a division: those the disaster happened to, and those it did not. The Concord Vigil requires an audience that is not itself bereaved. The Lent Seal requires a seal holder who has something left to lose. In the third winter of the Second Breath there was no such division anywhere in the Nine Domains. Everyone was the disaster. There was nobody left to perform grief at.

And then, in a dozen cities, people came out and stood in the streets with their hands open and empty at their sides, holding nothing, saying nothing. The Bureau has never been able to describe the event, because describing it requires a verb, and the crowds did not do anything.

**The method.** If the grievance cannot be suppressed and cannot be absorbed, it must be aimed.

**The instrument.** The Turned Mirror. Within sixty days of the reopening, the Bureau issued its finding that the Second Breath had been carried into the Nine Domains from beyond the passes, by a caravan out of the Unclaimed Territory, and that the Court's conduct throughout had been the defense of an empire against an injury done to it.

Consider what this asks of a citizen, because the design is exquisite. It asks nothing. There is no research to do, no archive to visit, no risk to run. A man who spent three winters behind a sealed courtyard door and lost his mother behind it can now be furious, loudly, in public, at no cost whatsoever, and be praised for it. His fury does not have to change. Only its direction.

**What it cost.** In Year 449 a river took a prefecture in the eastern plain. Travelers from the maritime powers happened to be present, asking questions in the street, and a crowd formed around them, and the crowd was not sent by anyone, which is the part the Bureau reports internally with something close to wonder.

Six months later the Court's own inquiry found that the prefecture had returned a death roll short by one hundred and thirty nine names. The finding was published. It was four pages into a fifty page report and no crowd formed around it.

**What your players can find.** The caravan. It is not difficult. There are eleven surviving members of it, they are in the Warrens, they have no residence codes, and they have been telling anyone who will listen for six years that they crossed the passes four months after the first physicians signed their statements. The dates are checkable. Nobody has checked them, because checking them is not dangerous, it is merely pointless, and pointlessness is a far more reliable defense than danger.

### The Fifth Reconstruction: The Empty Bench

**Issued the twentieth day of Hinge Gate, Year 452. Today.**

**The failure.** None. This is the first Reconstruction issued before a failure rather than after one, and that is precisely what it is.

**What happened.** On the seventeenth day of Hinge Gate a slope failed on the shoulder above Gate's End, on the shoulder that the empire and the Unclaimed Territory share, and came down across the pass road and the terraces below it in the space of about forty seconds. The Garrison at Iron Gate reached the site in nine hours, which is genuinely fast, and Marshal Hundred Battles has been at the site since, which is genuinely creditable.

The count is not known. It will not become known. Survey teams have been dispatched, foreign observers have been turned back at the Iron Gate road on grounds of avalanche risk, and the prefecture has been asked to submit its roll through the Bureau of Border Harmony, which is the bureau with the smallest staff and the longest queue in the Nine Domains, and everyone who chose that routing knew that.

**And then this.** On the nineteenth day, in one of the maritime powers, a contest house called the Alabaster Ground kept a silence before its game. Ten thousand people stood without speaking for one minute. The notice they raised read, in their own language: _in memory of those lost in the disaster on the border between our neighbors and Gate's End._

The Bureau of Harmonious Narrative circulated that notice throughout the Nine Domains on the twentieth day with one word removed. The word was border. The circulated text reads: _in memory of those lost in the disaster between our neighbors and Gate's End._

Then the Bureau published its finding: that a foreign house had named Gate's End alongside a foreign land as though the two stood in the same relation to the world, that this constituted an injury to the empire's integrity, and that an apology was required.

**The second charge.** The Bureau further found that the Alabaster Ground had used the name Gate's End at all. There is a decree of the eleventh month of Year 451, known as the Rectified Name, that the prefecture is to be written in the Court's own orthography, as the Gate of the Nine Domains, on the reasoning, which is never stated in the decree and never needs to be, that a place called Gate's End is a place where the empire ends.

**Why this is the fifth instrument and not merely a bad afternoon.** Every earlier Reconstruction required something. The One Lamp required a monopoly. The Ten Breaths required stories worth harvesting. The Lent Seal required a seal holder. The Turned Mirror required a foreigner to be present.

The Empty Bench requires nothing. The accused is nine hundred li beyond the passes, has no interest in the empire, cannot be summoned, cannot be examined, and cannot testify. The bench where the defendant would sit is empty, and it will stay empty, and this is not a weakness of the proceeding. It is the design. A defendant who cannot appear cannot say anything inconvenient, and a trial that cannot conclude does not have to.

**The Offered Quarrel.** Understand what the Bureau has actually done, because your players will not see it for some time and the moment they do see it should land like cold water.

On the twentieth day of Hinge Gate, the Nine Domains are arguing. In every teahouse in the Outer Market, in the Academic Quarter, in the guild halls, in the Warrens. Some are furious at the Alabaster Ground. Some are quietly delighted to point out that the Bureau's own bulletins, from as recently as last winter, print Gate's End in plain letters on the front page. Both sides are engaged. Both sides are researching. Both sides are enjoying themselves.

Nobody is asking how many people were on that slope.

The quarrel is not a failure of the Bureau's messaging. The quarrel is the product. It was manufactured, packaged, and handed to the empire two days after a mountain came down, and it was handed over precisely because it is arguable, because an argument that can be won is far more absorbing than a count that cannot be obtained.

**What your players can find.** Everything. That is the trap and you must run it as a trap.

The Bureau's own back bulletins print Gate's End hundreds of times. The Court's own maps print it. The decree of Year 451 has not been applied to the Archive because re glossing the back files is a task of some four million documents and the section responsible has nine people. A player with an afternoon and a reader's pass can assemble a devastating, unanswerable, fully evidenced demonstration that the Court is prosecuting a foreign contest house for using the Court's own current spelling.

And it will change nothing, and worse, publishing it will feed the quarrel, because the quarrel is the point and any contribution to it is a contribution.

The correct play is not to win the argument. The correct play is to refuse it, and go to Gate's End, and count. Let your players work that out for themselves. Do not have anyone tell them.

### The Sixth Reconstruction: The Ten Thousand Lamps

**In draft. Takes effect at the Hinge of Year 452, thirty days from the opening of your campaign.**

This is the spine of the game.

**The problem it solves.** All five existing Reconstructions share an assumption that the Bureau's own analysts now believe is obsolete: that the empire's attention is scarce and can be directed. The Ten Breaths, the Lent Seal, the Turned Mirror, all of them are instruments for pointing a finite quantity of public attention somewhere useful.

But the Qi lantern relays have kept growing. There are now more accounts of any given event than any person could read in a lifetime, and the Bureau has understood, before anyone outside it has, what this means.

**The method.** Do not dim the record. Drown it.

The One Lamp held the only light in the room. The Ten Thousand Lamps lights every lamp in the room at once, and every one of them is true, and every one of them is beautifully made, and there is no darkness anywhere, and nobody will ever again find the one thing that matters, because it is not hidden. It is merely one of ten thousand.

The Bureau's draft language is not cynical. Read it aloud at your table when the moment comes. It says that the people of the Nine Domains are entitled to a full account of every calamity that befalls them, and it means it, and it proposes to give them nine hundred full accounts of every calamity, and to make each one accurate.

**The second clause.** There is a second clause and it is the one that should frighten your table.

The draft proposes that the Concord Vigil be moved. Not abolished. Moved, from the ninth day after a calamity to the third, and that the rite as spoken on the third day be revised from mourning to thanksgiving, on the finding that the empire's response to a calamity is itself the event of significance and should be commemorated while feeling is at its height.

Under the Sixth Reconstruction, the victory over a disaster is declared before the bodies are recovered. This is not new. The Bureau found the precedent itself, in the Archive, and cites it in the draft's third appendix: forty one days after Scorch Field stopped answering, the Bureau's bulletin of that season was headed _Great Concord Achieved in the Southern Rectification._

The instrument has come back around to where it started, and the people drafting it can see this, and have written it down, and are proceeding.

**What is not in the draft.** Nobody has been able to establish who commissioned it. It carries no requesting seal. It arrived in the Bureau's drafting section under the Gloss, and the drafting section has assumed throughout that it originates with the Steward's household, and there is no evidence for this beyond the assumption itself.

There is a reading of the base text, held by exactly one person your players may eventually meet, in which the Sixth Reconstruction was not commissioned by anyone. In which it is a carry: an instruction from an older Key, executed in a register where it now means something its author never wrote.

The Celestial Book has held that reading for six months. She has not published it. She is trying to determine which is worse.

## The Vermilion Trade

The Third Reconstruction created an industry, and the industry is now the fourth largest employer in the Nine Domains, and almost nobody thinks of it as political.

In every other order that has ever tried to control what its people say, this work is a cost. Someone must be paid to listen, and the money comes out of the treasury, and the treasury notices. The Lent Seal inverted that. By making every seal holder liable, the Court moved the entire expense onto the people being read.

**The Reading Houses.** A teahouse cannot afford a reader. Two hundred teahouses can afford eleven. So the reading is contracted out, and the contractors gathered where the labor was cheapest and the capital nearest, which is why the trade began in Salt Gate prefecture, three days from Xiaoyuan by the river road, and why Salt Gate has been called the second capital for thirty years by people who are not joking.

Salt Gate became expensive, as places do. The trade moved. It is now in Low Kiln, in Long Ford, in Grey Wharf, and prefectural governors compete for it openly with abatements on the assembly tax, because a Reading House employs four thousand people who are all literate and none of whom will ever organize, since organizing is the specific thing they are employed to notice.

**The sieve makers.** There are at least forty houses in the empire that sell the instruments rather than the labor. They will sell a seal holder a graded set of Qi sieves calibrated to flag a written phrase, and a service contract to update the gradings each season, and their factors will advise you, courteously and for free, on staffing: two to three readers for every fifty thousand souls under your roof. It is a solved problem. There are manuals. There is a guild.

**Why this matters to the plot.** Because the Court did not build this. The Court permitted it, and it grew, and it now has its own interests, and those interests are not the Court's.

The Vermilion Trade requires calamity the way a mill requires water. Each new season of gradings is a fee. Each Harmonization Audit is a hiring season. The Sixth Reconstruction, which will require every account of every calamity to be produced in the hundreds and each one verified, is the largest single expansion of the trade in its history, and the houses of Low Kiln and Grey Wharf know it, and have known it for longer than the Bureau's own drafting section has.

Somebody outside the Court read the draft first. Your players can find out who. It is one of the better threads in the campaign and it does not lead where they will expect, because it does not lead to a conspiracy. It leads to a factor in Grey Wharf who bought advance information the way he buys advance information about grain, and who does not know what he set in motion, and who will be genuinely upset when he learns.

## The Ledger of Fifteen

There is a document in the Iron Calculation's own archive that the school has never suppressed and has never circulated. It is eleven pages of arithmetic by a surveyor whose name is given only as an office number, and it establishes two things.

The first is that a stone laid in the dry season spares fifteen laid in the flood. The figure has been checked. It is, if anything, conservative.

The second is the part that has kept it in a drawer for forty years. The surveyor went through three hundred years of promotion records and found that no official in the history of the Nine Domains has ever been elevated for a flood that did not happen.

You cannot be promoted for an absence. A prefect who spends nine years and a fortune keeping his levees sound presides over nine uneventful years, and uneventful years are what a mediocre prefect also produces. A prefect whose levees fail stands in the water to his waist for ten hours with the lantern relays watching, and is seen, and is elevated.

The Court is not indifferent to disaster. The Court is superbly, expensively, sincerely responsive to disaster, and this is exactly the problem, because responsiveness is the only part of the work that can be observed, and the empire promotes what it can observe.

Iron Calculation knows this. It is the single deepest reason he is the way he is, and if your table earns his trust he will say it plainly: the empire is not being destroyed by wickedness. It is being destroyed by an incentive that nobody designed and everybody obeys.

## The Ledger Closing

Here is the floor of the plot. Do not let your players reach it early, and when they reach it, do not explain it. Let them assemble it.

In Bone Script's valley the old people did not call the Dusk a corruption. They called it the ledger closing, and they said the land had been asked to give more than it recorded, for a long time, and that eventually the difference has to come from somewhere. They said it forty years before the Bureau noticed anything.

Set that against what the Reconstruction is. The Reconstruction is, in its entirety, an instrument for recording less than was taken.

The Court's own position is the reverse and is stated in every Orthodoxy catechism: the Dusk is a wound from the founding and the sealing, and the record has nothing to do with it. The Court is not lying. The Court believes this, and the evidence for it is strong, and it may well be true.

But hold these three facts beside each other and let them sit.

Scorch Field was struck from the gazetteer in Year 414. Its survivors could not renew papers, could not claim relief, could not be surveyed, because there was no lawful place for them to be from. The Dusk took the ground behind them faster than it has taken any ground since.

Nine Willow Bend was struck in Year 441. Bone Script carries four thousand and one names on bone because there is no other copy.

And there are cook fires in Nine Willow Bend. People have gone back into a Dusk zone and are not dying. Bone Script is the only person in the capital who knows, and he understands exactly what entering it into any record would summon.

The relation between the record and the world is the campaign's real question, and the honest answer is that nobody in the setting knows, including the Bureau, including the Celestial Book, including you. What is certain is narrower and worse: an erased prefecture receives no survey, no relief, no stabilization and no grain. Whatever the Dusk is, the Reconstruction is what decides who faces it alone.

The Court did not need the record to cause the disaster. It only needed the record to decide who would be helped, and it has been deciding that for eighty one years, and it has been calling the decision reconstruction.

## The Thirty Days

Your campaign opens on the twentieth day of Hinge Gate, Year 452. The Hinge is thirty days away. Three things happen on the first intercalary day, and they happen together.

**The Key turns.** Every glossed document in the empire becomes a document about something slightly different. Anything your players have decoded expires. Anything they have not yet decoded becomes harder. Any instruction they have set in motion and not yet completed becomes a carry, and a carry does what nobody intended.

**The Sixth Reconstruction takes effect.** The Concord Vigil moves to the third day and becomes thanksgiving. Every calamity after the Hinge will be declared a victory before it has been counted, permanently, as a matter of standing procedure.

**The Gate's End roll is due.** The prefecture must submit through the Bureau of Border Harmony. If it submits nothing, the disaster has no dead. If it submits a number, the number is the number, forever.

Thirty days. That is your clock and you should show it to the table on the first evening. Let them see the shape of it. There is no version of this campaign in which they stop the Hinge from arriving.

## Running the Reconstruction

**What the players are.** They are the hands. Not witnesses, not rebels, not investigators by trade. They are Eighth Rank copyists in the Bureau of Harmonious Records, and the Gate's End file is going to cross their desk in the first session, and they are going to have to do something with it, and every option is a real option with a real cost.

They can process it correctly. This is not a trap and you must not punish it. Processing it correctly is what a decent, frightened person does, and the campaign works perfectly well if they do.

**Scenes that work.**

- A glossed instruction they must execute before they have finished reading it.
- The seal holder at the teahouse they have used for years, who now has to decide about them.
- The Concord Vigil, run straight, with no irony, with a player's own character genuinely moved. This scene should be beautiful. It is much worse if it is beautiful.
- A factor from Grey Wharf offering a legitimate, well paid, entirely legal contract.
- The moment a player realizes the argument they have been winning for three sessions was issued to them.

**The schools.** Golden Orthodoxy drafted the Sixth and does not understand what it has drafted. Iron Calculation built the relays that broke the One Lamp and has never been forgiven for it. Crimson Lineage remembers when there was no Reconstruction and is the only faction that can say so with authority. Common Flame is the target of the Empty Bench and does not yet know it. Verdant Path holds the Silt Papers lineage and has been quietly correct for eighty one years and has been punished for it every time. Bright Mirror will enforce the Sixth exactly as written, which is the most dangerous thing anyone in this setting can do.

**What the table must not be told too early.** That the Sixth may be a carry. That the cook fires exist. That the Bureau is not lying. Especially that last one. Let them spend a dozen sessions hunting for the lie, and let them find, instead, an arrangement of true things, assembled by tired people working from summaries, that has killed more of the empire than the Dusk has.

- The Silt Papers are in the west wing and have not been requested in thirty years. A request is logged.
- Eleven caravan survivors in the Warrens have been telling the truth for six years. Their dates are checkable in an afternoon.
- Someone in Grey Wharf read the Sixth Reconstruction before the Bureau's own drafting section finished it.
- The Celestial Book has a reading of the base text and has held it for six months and is trying to determine which answer is worse.
