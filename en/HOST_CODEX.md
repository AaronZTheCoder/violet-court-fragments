# The Violet Court Fragments

## Host's Codex

> _For the Host's eyes only. This codex contains the structural frameworks, the deeper currents, and the tools needed to bring the Violet Court to life at your table. Guard it well. The players should never read it._

---

## Part One: The Host's Vocation

### The Way of the Host

You are not the players' adversary. You are not a computer executing programmed responses. You are the world's senses: you describe what the characters see, hear, smell, and feel. You are every person they meet, from the lowest street sweep to the highest Grand Council elder. You are the Crimson Dusk's slow advance and the sudden terror of a Harmonization Audit. Most importantly, you are the players' collaborative partner in telling a story none of you could have told alone.

Your job is not to defeat the players. It is to present them with hard choices, to ensure those choices have consequences, and to help the table discover what happens when people with competing visions of the good are forced to share a dying empire.

### Principles

**Be a fan of the characters.** Want them to succeed, even as you place obstacles in their path. The most satisfying victories demand real effort.

_In Practice._ Consider a player whose character, a junior scribe from the Outer Court, decides to forge a document to gain access to the Grand Council's restricted records. The player rolls well and succeeds. Instead of saying "you forge the document and get inside," describe it as a triumph. "Your brush moves with a confidence you did not know you possessed. The seal comes out perfect. The guard barely glances at it. You step into the corridor and realize your heart is pounding. You are inside. No one has ever done this before." Let them savor that victory. Then let the consequences arrive. When things later go wrong because of this act, and they should go wrong because every act has consequences, the player will remember that moment of triumph. They will work twice as hard to survive because they earned that moment. Being a fan means you want the characters to succeed so that their failures carry weight. When they fall, it matters because you and they both wanted them to soar.

**Let the dice matter.** When a roll is called for, respect the result. If you are not willing to accept failure, do not call for a roll. Narrate the success and move on.

_In Practice._ The players are negotiating with a minor official who holds a crucial piece of information. The player makes an argument so compelling that the entire table is convinced. You are tempted to skip the roll and grant success because the player's words were excellent. Do not skip the roll. The character's skill at persuasion is separate from the player's skill at rhetoric. Call for a Presence plus Persuasion roll against TN 15. If they succeed, the official nods and hands over the document. If they fail, the official smiles apologetically and says "I wish I could help, but my hands are tied." Now the players must find another way. That failure creates a new avenue of play. It is not a punishment. It is a branch in the story. The reverse also applies. If a player gives a weak argument but rolls well, the character's natural charm carries the day despite the player's fumbling words. Describe how the character finds exactly the right phrasing, saving face for both parties. The dice are the game's engine of surprise. They introduce outcomes no one at the table predicted. That unpredictability is the source of the game's most memorable moments.

**Prepare situations, not plots.** A plot says "the players will go to the archive and find the document." A situation says "a document that could destroy a Grand Council member is hidden in the archive, and three different factions are looking for it." The players decide how to engage with the situation. You react.

_In Practice._ You have prepared a situation: a disgraced scholar possesses a journal containing evidence that the current Director of the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative concealed an eyewitness account of the founding crime. The scholar is hiding in the Warrens, the labyrinthine slums beneath the capital. Two factions, the Golden Orthodoxy and the Iron Calculation, know the scholar exists and want the journal. The Golden Orthodoxy wants to destroy it to preserve the narrative. The Iron Calculation wants to use it as leverage. Now the players enter the scene. They could find the scholar and bargain for the journal. They could sell the location to one of the factions for a price. They could let the factions fight while they slip in and steal it. They could protect the scholar and become a target themselves. They could forge a copy and sell it to both factions. You have no idea what they will do. That is the point. Your preparation is not a script. It is a sandbox with moving parts. When the players act, you determine how the factions react. The scholar reacts. The environment reacts. The story emerges from the collision between player agency and your prepared situation. This approach requires trust. You must trust that the players will engage with interesting situations. They, in turn, must trust that you will honor their creative solutions. When that trust exists, the game becomes something none of you could have designed alone.

**The Court is a web, not a ladder.** Every character is connected to others through obligation, history, and interest. When the players pull on one thread, the whole web trembles. Track who owes what to whom.

_In Practice._ The players have discovered that a clerk of middle rank in the Ministry of Rites is secretly funneling information to the Verdant Path. They decide to confront the clerk. Before the session, you check your relationship map. The clerk is the nephew of the Iron Calculation's chief accountant. The chief accountant owes a favor to the Night Warbler. The Night Warbler has been investigating the same clerk independently. This means that when the players confront the clerk, the web reacts. The clerk threatens them with his uncle's position. The uncle learns of the confrontation within hours. The uncle asks the Night Warbler for information about the players. The Night Warbler now knows the players are interested in the same target he is pursuing. He may approach them as a potential ally or as a rival, depending on his assessment of their usefulness. The players' simple act of confronting a clerk has rippled through four layers of the Court. You did not plan this. The web produced it naturally. This is why you maintain the map. This is why you update it between sessions. The web is the Court's immune system. When the players act, the web responds in ways that feel organic because the connections exist independently of any single scene.

**The Dusk does not negotiate.** The Crimson Dusk is the one force in the game that cannot be reasoned with, bribed, or outmaneuvered. It advances at the pace you decide. Use it to create urgency when the intrigue grows too comfortable.

_In Practice._ The players have spent three sessions navigating Court politics, building alliances, and accumulating Celestial Favor. They have become comfortable. They have begun to think of the Court as a game they can win. Now is the time to remind them of the stakes. Describe a messenger arriving at dawn, covered in dust, having ridden through the night. A border town has fallen to the Dusk. Not a strategic town. Not a major holding. Just a small farming community that existed on the edge of the empire's notice. But it was a week's travel closer to the capital than the last town that fell. The players watch as the messenger delivers his report to a bureau official who waves dismissively and files the report in a growing stack of similar reports. The official says "these things happen. The border has always been unstable." He is wrong. The players know he is wrong. The Dusk is not an invading army. It is not a political faction. It is reality unmaking itself. You cannot negotiate with a wound. You cannot bribe decay. When the Dusk advances, all the political games become suddenly trivial. The players must choose: continue their schemes or respond to the existential threat. The best campaigns let them do both, because that is where the hardest choices live. Do I save my ally from the Audit or do I save the village from the Dusk? The answer should never be easy.

**Every truth has a cost.** The game is full of secrets. When players uncover one, ask yourself: who does not want this known? What will they do to bury it? The discovery is the beginning of the story, not the end.

_In Practice._ The players have found evidence that the current Star of the Court, the figurehead leader of the Grand Council, was not elected but installed by a cabal within the Golden Orthodoxy. This is a significant discovery. The player who found it is excited, proud of their investigative work. This is the moment when many Hosts would let the discovery stand as a victory. Instead, pause and ask who is harmed by this truth. The Golden Orthodoxy's leadership would be destroyed if this became public. The Star herself would be revealed as a puppet. The Bright Mirror would have grounds to open a focused inquiry into the appointment and everyone who arranged it. All of these parties now have reason to act. Within a day of the players' discovery, they receive a polite invitation to dinner from a Golden Orthodoxy minister. A junior clerk, visibly terrified, slips a note under their door warning them to drop the investigation. An attempt is made on the life of a contact who helped them find the evidence. The players now possess a truth that is also a target painted on their backs. They must decide what to do with it. Publishing it would destroy the Golden Orthodoxy and destabilize the Court. Suppressing it would protect them but betray their principles. Trading it would make them powerful but complicit. The truth is not a reward. It is a responsibility. Make the players feel its weight.

### Common Host Mistakes

Even experienced Hosts make errors. Here are the most common pitfalls specific to the Violet Court and how to avoid them.

**Mistake One: Overpreparing the Court.** You create detailed biographies for fifty minor officials. You map every corridor of the Bureau of Harmonious Records. You have schedules, routines, and backup plans. The players spend an entire session talking to one clerk and then leave for the Outer Market, never to return. This is not a waste. It is the natural result of player agency. Prepare situations, not floorplans. For minor officials, the NPC Quick Generator in Part Seven is sufficient. Create depth for the figures who matter. Let the rest be improvised. Your improvisation will be better than your preparation because it will respond directly to what the players are doing.

**Mistake Two: Overusing Audits.** Harmonization Audits are the Violet Court's signature event. They are also devastating to the players' resources and relationships. If you run an Audit every four sessions, they cease to be special. The players become numb to them. They stop caring about Ritual Harmony because they assume they will be punished regardless. Limit Audits to two or three per campaign. Let them be watershed moments that change the direction of the story. An Audit that arrives after ten sessions of careful play has ten times the impact of an Audit that arrives after three.

**Mistake Three: Making the Crimson Dusk a Background Detail.** The Dusk is easy to forget. It is distant, abstract, and advances slowly. If you only mention it when it spreads across the map, the players will treat it as weather: something that happens outside their concern. Bring the Dusk into every session, even briefly. A refugee in the market. A report of a Dusk Touched oracle speaking prophecy. A shipment of supplies being diverted to the front. A character's dream of red light seeping under a door. The Dusk should be a constant presence, like a low note that never stops playing.

**Mistake Four: Forgetting the Web.** When players interact with a figure, the other figures in the web should react. If the players meet with the Guest Among Forests, the Bright Mirror should learn of it. If the players help the Iron Calculation, the Golden Orthodoxy should take notice. The simplest way to implement this is to end each session by asking yourself: which three figures in the web would care about what just happened? Then have them act between sessions. A message arrives. A favor is called in. A relationship on the map changes color. The players learn that their actions have observers.

**Mistake Five: Making Every Official a Hostile Bureaucrat.** The Court is full of petty tyrants and obstructionists. It is also full of people who are kind, afraid, corruptible in interesting ways, or genuinely trying to do good within a broken system. If every official the players meet is a sneering obstacle, the world feels cartoonish. Vary the interactions. Some officials are helpful because they are naive. Some are helpful because they see an opportunity. Some are helpful because they are being used by a larger player. Surprise the players with kindness from unexpected quarters. It makes the cruelty more real when it arrives.

**Mistake Six: Ignoring Player Backstories.** The character creation chapter encourages players to embed their characters in the Court's fabric. A player who chose the "family indebted to the Crimson Lineage" background has given you a hook. Use it. Have a member of the family call in the debt. Have a rival family emerge seeking revenge. Have the Iron Bone mention in passing that he knew the player's grandfather. Every detail in a character's backstory is a gift. It is an invitation to make the world personal. A threat to a character's family is more compelling than a threat to a random village. A revelation about a character's past is more powerful than a revelation about a stranger's.

**Mistake Seven: Resisting Player Ideas.** A player says "I want to start a rumor that the Bright Mirror is corrupt. I have a plan." Your instinct might be to protect your vision. You have prepared the Bright Mirror as an incorruptible figure. A rumor that he is corrupt undermines that. Resist the instinct to say no. Instead, ask: how do you do this? Who do you approach first? What evidence do you plant? The Court is a system of perception and belief. If the players invest resources in changing what people believe, let them. The Bright Mirror will eventually learn who started the rumor. He will respond. But the rumor itself can change the Court's dynamics in ways you never anticipated. The players will feel powerful. The Bright Mirror will become a more complex figure: someone whose reputation can be damaged despite his actual purity. This is interesting.

**Mistake Eight: Running the Game Alone.** The Violet Court is complex. You do not need to carry it alone. Delegate. Ask a player to track the relationship map. Ask another to manage the Celestial Favor tracker. If you are unsure about a rule, ask the table: "what feels right here?" The players are your collaborators. They want the game to be good. Lean on them. A Host who tries to control everything ends up controlling nothing. A Host who shares the burden ends up with a table of invested, engaged players who shape the story together.

## Part Two: The Deeper Currents

### Reading the Water

_This section explains the structural dynamics of the Celestial Court. It is written in the language of the game world. No other language is needed. The patterns described here are universal: any system of concentrated power, sustained over centuries, develops certain recurring shapes. If you recognize those shapes from other contexts, you are reading the water correctly._

### The Shape of Power

Power in the Celestial Court flows through four channels:

**Narrative.** Whoever controls the official record controls what can be said. The Bureau of Harmonious Narrative does not merely spread information. It defines what counts as information and what counts as heresy. The Alchemist knows this better than anyone, which is why he drinks.

**Purity.** The Commission for Celestial Purity enforces orthodoxy. But orthodoxy is a moving target. What was acceptable last year is suspect this year. What is suspect this year is treason next year. The Bright Mirror serves an ideal. The ideal is a weapon that anyone can pick up.

**Calculation.** The empire runs on resources. Qi, grain, labor, information. The Iron Calculation school controls the flows. They are indispensable. Indispensability is a form of power that no amount of doctrine can override. The Iron Calculation knows this. So does everyone who resents him for it.

**Blood.** The Eight Great Families were there at the founding. They remember what the Court's official histories have erased. The Crimson Lineage's power is not ideological. It is generational. They do not argue doctrine because they are the reason doctrine exists. The Duke of Eternal Night is the last of his generation. When he goes, something irreplaceable goes with him.

### The Cycle

The Court's internal dynamics follow a pattern that has repeated for three hundred years:

1. **Accumulation.** A school or figure accumulates too much influence. Favors owed, secrets known, resources controlled. The balance becomes uncomfortable.

2. **Correction.** A Harmonization Audit is triggered. The dominant figure is cut down. Their allies scramble. Their enemies feast. The balance is restored, for a time.

3. **Fragmentation.** The victorious coalition fragments. Old allies become new rivals. The cycle begins again.

The players enter this cycle as minor figures. Their goal initially is simply to not be ground up by it. Later, they may learn to ride it. Eventually, they may ask whether the cycle itself can be broken.

### The Six Schools as Worldviews

Each school is not merely a school of thought. It is a coherent answer to the question: "How should the empire be governed in the Emperor's absence?"

**The Golden Orthodoxy** believes in continuity. The old ways worked. Deviation invites disaster. The Crimson Dusk is proof that they were right. Their weakness: the old ways demonstrably do not work against the Dusk. Their rituals fail. Their doctrine cannot explain what is happening. A worldview built on the premise that you already have all the answers cannot survive contact with a question you have never seen before.

**The Verdant Path** believes in adaptation. The empire must change or die. Decentralization, openness to nonhuman peoples, reform of the Court's most rigid structures. Their weakness: they are always one Audit away from annihilation. Their ideas are popular. Popularity is threatening.

**The Iron Calculation** believes in optimization. Governance is engineering. Doctrine is a variable to be tuned. If the Court would simply let them run the empire as a machine, everything would work. Their weakness: people are not machines. A system optimized for efficiency rather than humanity eventually grinds the humanity out of everyone in it.

**The Crimson Lineage** believes in inheritance. Rule is in the blood. The families who built the empire should govern it. They are not wrong that institutional knowledge matters. They are not wrong that the Court's doctrinal Harmonization Audits destroy competence as often as corruption. Their weakness: they are dying. Their children do not want the burden. Their grandchildren barely know the old names.

**The Common Flame** believes in the people. The Court exists to serve, not to rule. When it forgets this, the people have the right to remind it. With fire, if necessary. Their weakness: rage is not a governance plan. Burning down the old order is easy. Building something better is hard. The wolf does not build.

**The Bright Mirror** believes in purity. The Court's founding ideals were noble. The problem is that people keep falling short of them. If everyone could simply be made pure, the empire would heal. Their weakness: no one is pure. Not the Mirror himself, though he comes closer than anyone. A system that demands perfection from imperfect people eventually devours itself.

### What the Crimson Dusk Represents

_This section is for the Host's understanding. The players should discover it through play, or not at all._

The Crimson Dusk is not an external threat. It is a consequence. At the empire's founding, a ritual sacrificed or bound the land's original guardians and installed the Sun Emperor as their replacement, the new living conduit between Heaven and Earth. When the first generation of Court elders completed His sealing in the Spire one hundred and fifty five years ago, they severed that replacement conduit. The Crimson Dusk is the delayed wound left by both acts, a slow corruption of reality that became visible only later as the severed world tried to heal itself.

This truth is the game's central mystery. Different figures know different pieces of it:

- The Celestial Book knows everything. She has read the original charter. She knows what was done.
- The Duke of Eternal Night knows the Emperor is imprisoned. He was there when the vote was taken.
- The Guest Among Forests knows the Dusk is the delayed wound left by the founding ritual and the Emperor's completed seal, not a punishment for heterodoxy.
- The Iron Bone knows the Dusk first manifested beyond the border provinces in his penal camp, where concentrated suffering gave it a path inward.
- The Cinnabar Heart knows the Emperor's edicts are forged, though he does not know why.
- The Bright Mirror suspects the Court's corruption caused the Dusk and is investigating.

The players may never uncover the full truth. That is acceptable. The truth is a reward for engagement with the mystery, not a requirement for the game to be satisfying.

## Part Three: Running the Court

### Making the Court Feel Real

The Celestial Court should feel vast, ancient, and labyrinthine. Every official the players meet has a superior they fear and a subordinate they exploit. Every document carries the weight of precedent. Every chamber has witnessed a century of whispered deals and sudden betrayals.

### The Texture of Bureaucracy

Use these details to ground the Court in sensory reality:

- Ink stains on sleeves. Everyone writes constantly. Reports, memoranda, denunciations, confessions.
- Seals. Every document carries seals: the author's personal chop, the bureau's official stamp, the reader's verification mark. Forging a seal is a capital offense.
- Tea. Meetings are conducted over tea. The quality of the tea signals the importance of the meeting. Being served stale tea is an insult. Being served the host's private reserve is a declaration of trust.
- Waiting. Officials spend hours waiting in antechambers. The length of the wait signals your standing. Being seen immediately is a mark of high favor. Being kept waiting until sunset is a message.
- Corridors. The Court's buildings are a maze of connecting passages. Private conversations happen in corridors. Secrets are exchanged while walking. Standing still to talk suggests you have something to hide.

### The Six Schools in Play

When portraying figures aligned with each school, return to their core belief:

| School           | Core Belief                                  | How They Address You                                                                                 |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Golden Orthodoxy | "Preserve what the Founders built."          | Formally, using full titles. They are always evaluating your orthodoxy.                              |
| Verdant Path     | "The world changes. We must change with it." | Warmly, but with an edge of exhaustion. They have survived Harmonization Audits. They know the cost. |
| Iron Calculation | "Everything can be measured and improved."   | Efficiently. They value your time as much as theirs. They have no patience for ceremony.             |
| Crimson Lineage  | "Blood remembers what doctrine forgets."     | With ancient courtesy. They are patient. They have been playing this game for centuries.             |
| Common Flame     | "Power should serve the powerless."          | Directly, sometimes aggressively. They are testing whether you are real or just another courtier.    |
| Bright Mirror    | "Purity is not negotiable."                  | With unnerving intensity. They are always watching. They are always weighing.                        |

### Tracking the Web

The Host should maintain a simple relationship map. On a sheet of paper, write the names of the ten major figures. Draw lines between them labeled with the nature of their connection: "owes favor," "blackmail material," "secretly allied," "former lovers," "blood feud." When players interact with one figure, glance at the map and ask: who learns about this? Who reacts?

This web is a living thing. The players' actions add new lines and erase old ones. By the end of a campaign, the map should look entirely different from how it began.

### The Bureau of Harmonious Records

_Read aloud when the players first approach:_

"The Ministry rises before you like a cliff face of pale stone and dark timber. Seven stories tall, its windows are narrow slits designed to keep out light and prying eyes. The entrance is a massive bronze door covered in the names of every Director who has served since the founding. The names begin at eye level, crisp and legible, and continue upward into the shadows where the oldest names have been worn smooth by weather and time. The air here smells of old paper, beeswax, and the faint metallic tang of ink that has oxidized over decades. You hear nothing from inside. The building absorbs sound the way sand absorbs water."

_What the players sense as they enter:_ The temperature drops noticeably. The corridors are narrow, forcing visitors to walk single file in places. The walls are lined with shelves that disappear into darkness overhead. Every surface is covered in writing: labels, warnings, classification marks, the personal notations of generations of archivists. The floor is worn concave in the center of each hallway from centuries of foot traffic. The silence is oppressive. Archivists move without speaking, exchanging documents with nods.

_Interactive elements:_

1. The Index Keeper: A gaunt woman with fingers stained by ink who sits at a desk near the entrance. She does not ask what the players want. She asks what they are authorized to see. She knows every document's location and every loophole in the access rules. She can be reasoned with, bribed, or threatened, but she has seen it all before and is unimpressed by any of it. She responds best to those who treat the archive with reverence.
2. The Reading Room: A chamber with a high ceiling and long oak tables. Scholars and officials sit reading under the dim light of Qi lamps. A player who attempts to remove a document from this room triggers a silent alarm. The doors lock. Guards arrive within three minutes. The only way to avoid this is to have the Index Keeper's personal seal on the borrowing slip.
3. The Stacks: Deep within the Ministry, the shelves grow older and dustier. Some sections have not been visited in living memory. The oldest documents are written on materials that predate paper: silk, bamboo, bone. In these depths, the players may find things that were never meant to be found. They may also find things that have been waiting.

_Hidden secret:_ In the subbasement, behind a shelf that appears to be structural, there is a sealed chamber containing the original charter of the Court, predating the current founding documents by seventy years. It mentions a thirteenth school that was erased from all records. The twelfth school was the Alchemists. No one knows what the thirteenth was called.

### The Grand Council Chamber

_Read aloud when the players first enter:_

"You step through a curtain of heavy silk and the world changes. The Grand Council Chamber is a vast rotunda that could hold five hundred people standing. Its dome bears a vast mural of the empire's founding. The Sun Emperor stands above conquered chaos, the first elders kneel at His feet, and the Nine Domains spread below like a garden. The mural has been retouched so often that some provinces vanish beneath newer paint. The floor is black marble polished to a mirror finish. You can see your own face looking up at you from beneath your feet. Seven seats of black jade stand in a circle, each carved from a single block. Four are occupied. Three are vacant and covered with white silk. The air is cold and still. You feel the weight of a hundred generations watching you."

_What the players sense as they enter:_ The room suppresses echoes with unnatural precision. A whisper reaches only its intended listener. A footstep sounds once and vanishes. The temperature is several degrees colder than the corridor outside. The lighting comes from Qi crystals set into the walls, casting a pale blue glow that makes everyone look slightly ill. The Council members do not turn to face newcomers. They watch from the corners of their eyes. The silence when you speak is absolute. Every word lands clearly, exactly once, and is remembered.

_Interactive elements:_

1. The Speaker's Stone: A small raised platform in the center of the floor. Only those standing on it are formally recognized. Off the stone, your words are not part of the record. On the stone, everything you say is transcribed by scribes in the alcoves above. Players must decide: speak safely from the floor, or commit to the record from the stone.
2. The Alcoves of Record: High in the walls, behind grilles, sit the Scribes of the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative. They write everything. They never stop. They never react. If a player looks up at them, one scribe may pause and make a note of the player's name. This note goes into a permanent file.
3. The Vacant Seats: Three black jade chairs stand beneath white silk. A player who approaches them will be stopped by guards without specific authorization. Each vacancy has an official explanation: illness, retirement, or ritual seclusion. None of the explanations accounts for why the silk has gathered decades of dust.

_Hidden secret:_ Beneath the Speaker's Stone, there is a hollow space accessible only by a mechanism that requires three specific Council members to activate simultaneously. Inside is a sealed urn containing the ashes of the first Alchemist, who was executed after the vote to imprison the Emperor. His dying words were carved into the urn's lid: "You have sealed the sky. You will never see it clear again."

### The Outer Market

_Read aloud when the players first arrive:_

"The Outer Market erupts around you before you see it. You hear it first: a roar of voices haggling, shouting, laughing, crying out prices and warnings and greetings in a dozen dialects. Then the smell hits you: roasting meat, incense, fresh fish, sweat, spices, the sweet rot of overripe fruit, the chemical tang of Qi enhancement pills being cooked in open pots. The market is a sea of canvas awnings in every color, stretching across a plaza the size of a lake. Thousands of people move through it: merchants, laborers, soldiers, thieves, pilgrims, children, monks, foreigners from beyond the empire's borders. A man balancing a tower of cages filled with songbirds weaves through the crowd. A woman with Dusk scars on her arms sits motionless behind a table of charms. A child tugs at your sleeve and offers to guide you to someone who can get you anything. For a price. Everything has a price here."

_What the players sense as they enter:_ The sheer density of bodies is overwhelming. Strangers press against you from all sides. Hands brush your pockets. Eyes track your movements. The noise makes it hard to think. The ground is sticky with spilled drinks and crushed fruit. Beneath the chaos, there is a rhythm. The market has its own economy, its own laws, its own language. Those who learn to read it find opportunity. Those who do not find trouble.

_Interactive elements:_

1. The Information Brokers: At a permanent stall near the market's center, a tea seller named Old Feng also sells information. His prices vary based on your need. If he thinks you are desperate, he charges more. If he thinks you are dangerous, he charges less. Old Feng knows everyone and forgets nothing.
2. The Charm Seller: An old woman whose stall is covered in talismans, bones, and small bottles of colored liquid. Her charms may or may not work. She is honest about this. "Some work. Some do not. The difference is belief, not quality." She knows things about the Dusk that she should not know. She will not explain how.
3. The Auction Platform: At the south end of the market, rare goods are auctioned daily. Anything can appear: a confiscated scroll from the Archives, a Dusk Touched artifact, a prisoner of war, a secret. Players who watch the auctions learn a great deal about what the Court values and what it fears.

_Hidden secret:_ Beneath a particular fruit stall there is a trapdoor leading to a tunnel network that connects to the Bureau of Harmonious Records, the Grand Council Chamber, and the Spire of Eternal Vigil. The tunnel was built by the Alchemists three hundred years ago. The current owner of the fruit stall is a descendant who has been paid to keep the secret by an unknown patron. The patron changes every few decades. No one currently alive knows who the patron is.

### The Teahouse of Whispered Petitions

_Read aloud when the players first approach:_

"The Teahouse of Whispered Petitions is easy to miss. It sits on a narrow street off a narrow alley, its entrance no wider than a door. There is no sign. You would walk past it a hundred times without noticing. But those who know look for the single red lantern hanging above the door, always lit, even at midday. Inside, the teahouse is a single long room lit by paper lanterns that cast a warm golden light. The floor is raised tatami. The walls are simple, undecorated white plaster. There is no furniture except low tables and cushions. It is always quiet, even when full. People do not come here to be heard. They come here to speak without being overheard. The proprietress is a woman in middle age named Mistress Lien who moves with the grace of someone who was once a warrior. She remembers every face that has crossed her threshold. She has never repeated a confidence."

_What the players sense as they enter:_ The quiet is the first thing you notice. The street noise vanishes when the door closes. The air smells of jasmine tea and incense. The cushions are worn soft by years of use. Other patrons do not look up when you enter. They have learned not to. Mistress Lien greets each guest with a slight bow and a question: "Would you prefer a table near the window or near the fire?" The window tables are for those who want to be seen. The fire tables are for those who do not. The distinction is never explained. It is simply understood.

_Interactive elements:_

1. Mistress Lien: She is the teahouse's greatest asset and its greatest danger. She knows more secrets than the Celestial Book. She will never betray a confidence directly. But she drops hints. She gives warnings. She steers conversations in ways that benefit her unseen patrons. Players who earn her trust gain access to the most valuable commodity in the Court: reliable information. Players who offend her find that doors close across the entire capital.
2. The Walls Have Ears: Some of the teahouse's patrons are not patrons. They are listeners employed by various schools. A player who speaks too openly at a window table will find their words have traveled before their tea goes cold. The fire tables are safer but not safe. Only the private room in the back, which Mistress Lien grants only to those she trusts implicitly, is truly secure.
3. The Message Drop: A regular patron who sits at the same table every evening is a courier for the Underground Network. Players who leave a specific token on their table will receive a message in return. The token changes weekly. The password changes daily. The Underground Network has survived four Harmonization Audits. They are very, very good at staying hidden.

_Hidden secret:_ Behind the wall of the private room, there is a listening post built by the Alchemists and rediscovered by the Bright Mirror. The Bright Mirror has been monitoring every conversation held in that room for the past three years. He has never acted on what he has heard. He is waiting. He does not know what he is waiting for. But he will know when it arrives.

### The Spire of Eternal Vigil (Exterior)

_Read aloud when the players first see it:_

"The Spire of Eternal Vigil dominates the capital's skyline. It is not a building. It is a wound in the shape of a building, a needle of white stone that drinks the light around it at the exact center of the Celestial Court. No window breaks its surface. No door marks its base. It is seamless, as though it were carved from one unbroken piece of pale stone. The Spire is taller than anything around it. Taller than the Grand Council Chamber. Taller than the Imperial Palace. Taller than anything has a right to be. The sky around it is always strange. Clouds do not cross it. Birds do not fly near it. Rain falls around it in a perfect circle, as though the Spire itself repels water. At night, a faint red light pulses beneath the white surface, slow and regular, like an ember under ash. The people of the capital do not look at the Spire. They have learned not to. Those who stare too long develop headaches. Those who stare longer develop nosebleeds. Those who stare longest develop questions that the Commission for Celestial Purity does not want them to ask."

_What the players sense as they approach:_ The closer you get, the more wrong it feels. The stone gives off a steady warmth even in winter, yet the hair on your arms rises. A pressure builds behind your eyes. You hear a low hum that you feel in your teeth more than your ears. It is the combined chant of the thirty six monks at the base, carried through the stone. The perimeter guards face outward and watch every visitor. Their task is not mysterious. They prevent anyone from touching the seal or interrupting the chant.

_Interactive elements:_

1. The Perimeter: A line of polished black stones marks the boundary that unauthorized persons cannot cross. The boundary is not physical. It is a Qi barrier. Those who cross it without authorization feel their Qi drain rapidly. If they do not retreat within thirty seconds, they collapse. The guards do not intervene unless someone continues toward the chanting circle.
2. The Thirty Six Monks: Thirty six Golden Orthodoxy monks maintain the chant begun when the final seal was completed in Year 297. Six chant at any given time while the others rest, eat, study, or prepare to take their place. The sound has not stopped for one hundred and fifty five years. The oldest monks can speak while sustaining their part of the chant, a discipline called Forked Voice.
3. The Celestial Book's Balcony: From the Archive tower, which is the only building that overlooks the Spire's perimeter, there is a balcony where the Celestial Book sometimes stands. She does not watch the Spire. She watches those who watch the Spire. If a player catches her eye, she raises her hand in a gesture that could be a greeting or a warning. It has never been clear which.

_Hidden secret:_ The Spire is a prison. The first generation of Court elders sealed the Sun Emperor inside against His will and completed the outer seal in Year 297. At the founding, the sacrifice or binding of the land's original guardians made the Emperor a replacement living conduit between Heaven and Earth. The final sealing severed that conduit. The Crimson Dusk is the delayed wound left by both acts. The chant maintains the prison, not an act of worship. The red pulse is the wounded world pressing against the broken channel, and the pressure grows stronger as the seal weakens.

## Part Four: Harmonization Audits

### Running an Audit

A Harmonization Audit is the Violet Court's signature set piece. It is a session where the accumulated weight of the players' choices comes due. Run well, it is the most memorable session of the campaign. Run poorly, it feels arbitrary and punishing.

### When to Trigger an Audit

Audits should occur at dramatic moments, not on a fixed schedule:

- After the players have achieved a significant victory (the Audit cuts them down)
- When factional tensions have reached a breaking point (the Audit resolves them, violently)
- When the players have grown too comfortable (the Audit reminds them where they are)
- When a major figure needs to be removed from play (the Audit is the mechanism)

A campaign should feature two or three Audits total. More than that diminishes their impact.

### Audit Structure

A Harmonization Audit has four phases:

**Phase One: The Whisper.** Three to five days before the Audit is formally announced, rumors begin. The players hear that names are being collected. They see officials burning documents. They receive nervous visits from allies asking if they have heard anything. This phase is for preparation: players can hide evidence, call in favors, or make deals to improve their position.

**Phase Two: The Investigation.** The Audit is announced. Investigators from the Commission for Celestial Purity begin reviewing dossiers. Each player's Ritual Harmony determines their baseline risk. Their Faction Standing determines their protection. The players may be summoned for questioning. This is a social encounter: how they perform under interrogation affects the outcome.

**Phase Three: The Judgment.** Names are published. Some officials are cleared. Some are sanctioned (loss of position, loss of Celestial Favor, instruction). Some disappear. For each player, roll the Audit Resolution (see below). The results are public: everyone sees who survived and who did not.

**Phase Four: The Aftermath.** Power vacuums open. The survivors scramble to fill them. Alliances shift. Revenge is plotted. The players emerge changed, if they emerge at all.

### Audit Resolution

For each player, the Host determines a **Risk Level** based on the character's Ritual Harmony and Faction Standing:

| Ritual Harmony | Strong Protection | Moderate Protection | No Protection | Enemies       |
| -------------- | ----------------- | ------------------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| 60+            | Minimal Risk      | Low Risk            | Moderate Risk | High Risk     |
| 40 to 59       | Low Risk          | Moderate Risk       | High Risk     | Critical Risk |
| 20 to 39       | Moderate Risk     | High Risk           | Critical Risk | Terminal      |
| Below 20       | High Risk         | Critical Risk       | Terminal      | Terminal      |

**Risk Outcomes (roll d20):**

| Risk Level | Cleared | Sanctioned | Disappeared |
| ---------- | ------- | ---------- | ----------- |
| Minimal    | 5+      | 2 to 4     | 1           |
| Low        | 8+      | 3 to 7     | 1 to 2      |
| Moderate   | 12+     | 5 to 11    | 1 to 4      |
| High       | 16+     | 8 to 15    | 1 to 7      |
| Critical   | 19+     | 12 to 18   | 1 to 11     |
| Terminal   | 20 only | 15 to 19   | 1 to 14     |

**Cleared:** No consequences. Perhaps a warning.
**Sanctioned:** Loss of 2d10 Celestial Favor. Loss of 1d10 Ritual Harmony. Possible loss of position. Public humiliation.
**Disappeared:** The character is taken. The player creates a new character. This is how the first legacy cycle begins.

### Risk Baseline Definitions

The Risk table assumes the following definitions for Faction Standing:

**Strong Protection (Faction Standing 30+):** You have at least one faction that actively protects you. They will spend political capital, call in favors, and shield you from investigation. This protection is not absolute, but it is significant. A player who has cultivated a faction to this level should feel that investment paying off during an Audit.

**Moderate Protection (Faction Standing 10 to 29):** You have a faction that values you enough to offer some protection. They will not risk their own position for you, but they will speak on your behalf and may quietly intervene if the cost is low. The protection is real but limited.

**No Protection (Faction Standing -9 to +9):** You have no faction willing to protect you. You stand alone. Your fate depends entirely on your Ritual Harmony and your own actions during the Audit. This is the default state for most characters at the start of a campaign.

**Enemies (Faction Standing -10 or below):** A faction actively works against you. They will provide evidence, testimony, or rumors to the Commission. Having enemies does not guarantee you will be targeted, but it means someone with influence is actively trying to make you the target.

**Ritual Harmony Baseline.** A character's Ritual Harmony determines their row in the table. Ritual Harmony 60+ places a character in the safest band, reflecting a life aligned with the Court's expectations. Ritual Harmony 40 to 59 is the standard starting range, representing normal variance in adherence to doctrine. Ritual Harmony 20 to 39 reflects a pattern of deviation that has been noticed. Ritual Harmony below 20 indicates a character who lives at the edges of what the Court tolerates.

### Worked Example: Calculating Risk

Consider a character named Mara, a Bright Mirror with Ritual Harmony 48 and the following Faction Standing values: Golden Orthodoxy +5, Verdant Path +20, Iron Calculation 0, Crimson Lineage -12, Common Flame +15, Bright Mirror +10.

Mara's Ritual Harmony of 48 places her in the 40 to 59 band. Her best protection is +20 with the Verdant Path, which falls into Moderate Protection. However, she also has -12 with the Crimson Lineage, which means she has Enemies. The best protection determines the column, so Mara uses Moderate Protection, not Enemies.

On the table, comparing Ritual Harmony 40 to 59 with Moderate Protection gives a Risk Level of "Moderate Risk." This means on a d20, Mara is Cleared on 12+, Sanctioned on 5 to 11, and Disappeared on 1 to 4. She has a thirty five percent chance of being Sanctioned and a twenty percent chance of Disappearing. She will probably survive, but better than half her survival is owed to a single faction that has decided she is worth a word in the right room.

If Mara had Ritual Harmony 55 and had cultivated the Verdant Path to +30, she would have Strong Protection and face Low Risk (Cleared on 8+, Sanctioned on 3 to 7, Disappeared on 1 to 2). If she had Ritual Harmony 35 and Enemies, she would face Terminal Risk (Cleared on 20 only, Sanctioned on 15 to 19, Disappeared on 1 to 14). The wide range of possible outcomes shows why faction management and Ritual Harmony maintenance matter so much in the approach to an Audit.

### Adjusting Risk for Creative Player Actions

The standard Risk calculation assumes typical Audit participation. Players who take creative actions not covered by the standard options may affect their Risk in ways you adjudicate on the spot:

- **Providing actionable intelligence to investigators** (genuine assistance, not leverage or bribery): Reduce Risk by one level. The Commission rewards cooperation, even reluctant cooperation. A player who helps them understand a Dusk pattern or identify a genuine threat becomes less interesting as a target.

- **Harming the Commission's reputation** through public exposure of investigator misconduct: Increase Risk by two levels if the player is identified. The Commission protects its own. A player who makes the Commission look foolish or corrupt will not be forgiven quickly.

- **Using unconventional skills to aid the investigation** (Qi Theory to authenticate documents, Lore to identify ritual evidence, Craft to identify the tool that made a wound): Reduce Risk by one level if the contribution is significant and noted by a sympathetic investigator. The Commission does not value your skills directly, but they value results.

- **Invoking a senior patron** (a Grand Council member, a Twelve Figures ally): Reduce Risk by one level if the patron's intervention is credible and public. The patron will likely demand repayment later.

- **Drawing attention from the Bright Mirror**: If the Bright Mirror takes a personal interest in a player during the Audit, increase Risk by one level regardless of other factors. His interest is never casual.

- **Being caught in a lie to an investigator**: If the lie is discovered, increase Risk by one level. If the lie was about something directly relevant to the Audit, increase by two levels.

- **Making a deal with a Dusk Touched entity during the Audit**: Increase Risk by two levels. The Commission notices the taint on a character's Qi even if the deal was not observed.

The goal of these adjustments is to reward engagement with the Audit process. A player who takes creative actions rooted in the story should feel that their choices matter. A player who treats the Audit as an inconvenience to be waited out should feel the tension of passivity.

### Player Actions During an Audit

Players are not passive during an Audit. They may:

- **Call in Celestial Favor:** Spend 10 Celestial Favor to reduce Risk by one level. This represents calling in a debt from a powerful patron.
- **Produce Leverage:** If the player has blackmail material on an investigator, they may use it to reduce Risk by one level. The material is spent: once used, it cannot be used again.
- **Throw Someone Else to the Wolves:** A player may name another official (NPC or, if the table consents, another player) as a more deserving target. This is a Presence + Deception roll against TN 18. On success, the named target's Risk increases by one level while the player's Risk decreases by one. On failure, the player's Risk increases.
- **Flee:** The player may attempt to escape the capital before the Judgment. This is a series of skill checks (Stealth, Survival, Connections). On success, the character survives but loses all Faction Standing values and all Celestial Favor, and becomes a fugitive. This is functionally a new character but retains the same identity and knowledge.

### Example Audit: The Prepared Players

This example assumes the players saw the Audit coming and took steps to protect themselves.

**Phase One: The Whisper.** The session begins with a minor official the players befriended showing up at their residence late at night. His hands are shaking. He says he heard a name mentioned in the Commission corridors. Not the players' names. But a name connected to them. The players immediately begin preparing. One player visits the Iron Calculation and offers a future favor in exchange for a doctored resource allocation report that shows their department is essential to the war effort. Another player visits the Teahouse and leaves a token for the Underground Network, requesting information on which investigator has been assigned to their case. A third player burns several compromising documents in the garden brazier and buries the ashes. They are proactive. They do not wait to see what happens.

**Phase Two: The Investigation.** The Audit is announced targeting the Ministry of Rites, where two of the three players work. The lead investigator is a Justicar named Wei, known for her thoroughness and her incorruptibility. The players have learned through the Underground Network that Wei has a personal grudge against their section chief, who once insulted her in a Council session. This is useful: they can redirect her attention. When summoned for questioning, the player with the forged resource allocation report presents it with calm confidence. The player with the best social skills handles the interrogation, volleying Wei's questions with respectful deflections. The third player stays silent but watches for tells. When Wei asks directly about a discrepancy in last month's ledgers, the first player smoothly blames the now disgraced section chief. It is a lie. It is also plausible. Wei's grudge makes her receptive. She moves on.

**Phase Three: The Judgment.** When the names are published, all three players are Cleared. Their section chief is Sanctioned: demoted, publicly humiliated, and reassigned to a distant border post. The players breathe a collective sigh of relief. But they also notice something disturbing. The third player, who stayed silent during the interrogation, drew the attention of a different investigator. That investigator noted the player's name in a separate file. The file is not closed. It is merely pending.

**Phase Four: The Aftermath.** With their section chief gone, the players' department is in chaos. A power vacuum exists. The Iron Calculation contacts them: "You have proven competent. We have an opening in our department. The work is harder. The visibility is higher. The protection is stronger." The players must decide: accept the offer and deepen their ties to the Iron Calculation, or stay in their damaged department and rebuild. Either choice has consequences. Meanwhile, the third player learns that a Commission file with their name on it has been flagged for "future review." The Audit is over. Its shadow is not.

### Example Audit: The Unprepared Players

This example assumes the players ignored the warnings and were caught unprepared.

**Phase One: The Whisper.** Rumors of an imminent Audit reach the players through casual conversation. An ally mentions that the Commission has been unusually active. The players are busy pursuing their own goals: one is investigating a personal lead about the Dusk, another is courting a contact in the Golden Orthodoxy, the third is recovering from a failed Qi experiment. They acknowledge the rumor and file it away. They do not act. No documents are hidden. No favors are called. No deals are made. The Whisper fades into background noise.

**Phase Two: The Investigation.** The Audit is announced without warning. A scroll bearing the Commission's seal arrives at the players' residence at dawn. They are to present themselves for questioning within the hour. They arrive flustered and unprepared. The interrogation room is cold and windowless. The investigator is a Senior Justicar named Sheng, a man with a reputation for finding what you least want him to find. He has done his homework. He knows about the player's Dusk investigation. He knows about the failed Qi experiment. He knows about the meeting with the Golden Orthodoxy contact. He asks questions in a calm, unhurried voice, and he already knows the answers. The players stumble. They contradict each other. One player loses their temper and threatens Sheng, which is exactly the reaction he was hoping for. He notes it. The interview ends. The players leave knowing they have done poorly.

**Phase Three: The Judgment.** The names are published at sunset. One player is Sanctioned: loss of 15 Celestial Favor, loss of 8 Ritual Harmony, demoted from their current position to a filing clerk in a subbasement office that nobody visits. The second player is Sanctioned more lightly: a fine of 10 Celestial Favor and a formal warning placed in their permanent record. The third player, the one who threatened Sheng, has Disappeared. The other players search for them. They find nothing. The Commission denies knowing the name. The player's former residence has been sealed. It is as though they never existed. The third player's player must create a new character, beginning the legacy cycle.

**Phase Four: The Aftermath.** The surviving players are shaken. They have lost their colleague, their Faction Standing, and their momentum. The demoted player now works in a subbasement office where documents are sent to be forgotten. In that office, they discover something interesting: a stack of unresolved correspondence from a decade ago, involving someone whose name they recognize from the Court's founding records. The Sanction was devastating. But it has also placed the player exactly where a piece of the larger mystery is hidden. Every setback is also an opportunity. The players must now decide how to rebuild. They are bruised. They are diminished. They are not dead.

### At the Table: Running Your First Audit

The Harmonization Audit is the Violet Court's signature set piece. Running it well requires presence, pacing, and preparation. This section gives you concrete tools for making your first Audit feel tense, fair, and memorable.

**Audit Investigator Archetypes for the Host.**

The lead investigator sets the tone for the entire Audit. Choose or adapt one of these three archetypes for your first Audit.

_The Bureaucrat._ This investigator treats the Audit as paperwork. They are methodical, dispassionate, and utterly indifferent to the players as people. They ask questions from a prewritten list and write down every answer. They do not threaten. They do not offer deals. They simply process. The danger of the Bureaucrat is their inexorability: they will find every discrepancy, every inconsistency, because they check everything. Players can exploit this by feeding them meaningless details that bury the real issue in a mountain of information.

_The Believer._ This investigator genuinely believes in the Commission's mission. They are not corrupt. They are not cruel. They are convinced that purity is achievable and that Audits are necessary for the empire's health. They are polite, professional, and deeply dangerous. The Believer can be moved by genuine displays of integrity. A player who confesses a minor infraction voluntarily may earn the Believer's respect. A player who lies poorly will be pursued with the full force of someone who believes they are doing holy work.

_The Grudge._ This investigator has a personal reason to want the players to suffer. Perhaps a player offended a friend of theirs. Perhaps a player's faction is their enemy. Perhaps they simply dislike the look of the party. The Grudge is unfair by design. They ask leading questions. They ignore exculpatory evidence. They smile when the players stumble. The Grudge can be exposed, but doing so requires proof of bias. A player who catches the Grudge in a lie or a procedural violation can turn the entire Audit against the investigator, transforming a threat into an opportunity.

**What the Players Should Know Before Their First Audit.**

The characters live in the Court. They know what an Audit is. Do not let the players walk into an Audit blind because their characters would understand the basics. Before the Audit begins, tell the players what their characters would know. An Audit investigates doctrinal purity and factional loyalty. Outcomes range from Cleared to Sanctioned (loss of Celestial Favor and rank) to Disappeared (character removal). Having a powerful patron reduces risk. Having enemies increases it. Lying to an investigator is dangerous but sometimes necessary. Refusing to answer is a right that carries its own consequences. Give them this information in character through a nervous ally or a warning note. Players who understand the stakes make better choices, and better choices create better stories.

**A Minute by Minute Timeline for the Host During an Audit Scene**

Assume the players have been summoned for interrogation. The scene should take 20 to 30 minutes of table time at most.

**Minute 0 to 2: Arrival and Atmosphere.** Describe the Commission's waiting room. Cold. Windowless. A single Qi lamp that flickers. The players sit on hard wooden benches. No one speaks. They can hear footsteps in the corridor. A scribe passes without looking at them. The air smells of ink, old paper, and something metallic. Do not rush this. Let the silence build.

**Minute 3 to 5: The Call.** A name is called. One player is summoned first. The others wait, alone with their thoughts. If they ask to accompany, they are refused. "You will be called when it is your turn." This is the moment when individual pressure peaks.

**Minute 6 to 10: First Interrogation.** The lead investigator sits across from the player. A single sheet of paper is on the table. The investigator reads from it. The questions are specific, personal, and based on the player's actual actions in recent sessions. This is where you show that you have been paying attention.

**Minute 11 to 14: Pressure and Pivot.** The investigator leans forward. They set down the paper. They look at the player directly. "I am going to ask you a question. Think carefully before you answer." This is the question that tests everything. A player who tells the truth or a convincing lie passes through. A player who hesitates or contradicts themselves creates openings for the investigator.

**Minute 15 to 18: The Next Player.** The first player is sent back to the waiting room. The next is called. They do not learn what the first player was asked. The isolation continues.

**Minute 19 to 22: Group Questioning.** All players are brought back together. The investigator addresses them as a group. "You have given different answers to the same question. I want to hear you explain this discrepancy to each other." This is a test of loyalty and quick thinking.

**Minute 23 to 25: The Threat.** The investigator makes a statement that raises the stakes. "Someone in this room will not pass this Audit. The question is which one of you. I am giving you an opportunity to name the guilty party. If you do, the rest of you walk free." This is the moment when alliances break or hold.

**Minute 26 to 28: Resolution.** The investigator collects final statements. They stand. "You will receive the judgment within three days. You are dismissed." The players leave the room. The tension does not leave with them.

**Minute 29 to 30: The Release.** Outside, the corridor is empty. The door closes behind them. They can breathe again. For now.

**Five Things to Say When Players Freeze Up During an Investigation**

1. "Your character would know that silence is also an answer. The investigator is watching how you react as much as listening to what you say. You can refuse to answer. That is your right. But it comes with consequences."

2. "The investigator is not asking this question because they already know the answer. They are asking to see what you reveal in the way you answer. Take a breath. Decide what you want them to know."

3. "You have been in this room for three minutes. The investigator has not taken a note. They are not writing anything down. They are waiting for you to say something they cannot predict."

4. "Your character remembers a minor detail you might have overlooked. The clerk who escorted you in had ink on their left sleeve. That ink is the same color the Commission uses for classified documents. The investigator is not the only person in this building you could be talking to."

5. "The investigator just asked a question you cannot answer. That is fine. You can deflect. You can redirect. You can answer a different question entirely. The game here is not about the truth. It is about who controls the conversation."

**Four Examples of How Different Risk Outcomes Feel to the Player**

Each example is narrated in second person, as the Host would describe it to the player.

**Cleared (Minimal Risk).** "You step out of the interrogation chamber and the light hits you like a physical force. You had forgotten how bright the world is. The investigator said nothing memorable. They asked their questions. You gave your answers. They nodded. It felt like a transaction, not a trial. You reach the outer door and realize your hands are shaking. You did not know you were afraid until the fear left you. You are cleared. The Commission has no interest in you. But as you walk away, you hear your name spoken behind you, just once, in a voice you do not recognize. It could be nothing. It could be everything."

**Sanctioned (Moderate Risk).** "The investigator reads the judgment aloud without looking at you. It is a formality. The decision was made before you entered the room. Ten points of Celestial Favor are forfeited. Your rank is reduced one level. A formal letter of reprimand will be placed in your permanent file. You are assigned to a new post effective immediately: liaison to the grain accounting office in the Eastern District. It is a demotion. It is also a message. Someone wanted you moved. You do not know who. You will spend the next month finding out. The investigator slides a document across the table for your signature. Your pen scratches against the paper. It is the loudest sound in the room."

**Sanctioned (Critical Risk).** "The judgment is delivered not by the investigator but by a Commission herald, which means the decision came from above. You are not merely demoted. You are stripped of your rank entirely. Your Celestial Favor balance is confiscated. Your residence is sealed. You are given a new posting in a border supply depot, a place where officials go to be forgotten. The herald reads the judgment without inflection. 'You will report to your new post within seven days. Noncompliance will result in immediate Harmonization.' You are given a travel pass, a ration token, and a copy of the charges against you. The charges are fabricated. You know they are fabricated. So does the herald. So does everyone in the room. That does not change anything. You have three days to say your goodbyes. Three days to decide whether you will actually board that cart to the border."

**Disappeared (Terminal Risk).** "You are not given a judgment. You are given tea. The investigator pours it herself, a gesture that feels almost kind. She asks about your family, your health, your plans for the evening. You answer. You do not know why you are answering. The tea is warm. The room is quiet. Then you notice that the door behind you has been closed by someone you did not see enter. There are two guards where there was one before. The investigator is still smiling. She is still kind. 'There are some questions,' she says, 'that cannot be answered in a judgment chamber. We have a quieter place. We would like you to come with us.' You do not have a choice. You will not return. The people who know your name will slowly forget they ever knew it."

**A Checklist for What to Prep Before Running an Audit**

Before the session, confirm that you have the following ready:

- The Risk table with each player's Ritual Harmony and Faction Standing filled in.
- The name and personality of the lead investigator prepared.
- Three specific questions based on the players' recent actions. The questions should be grounded in what actually happened in play. Generic questions feel like filler. Specific questions feel like consequences.
- One piece of leverage for each player that they could potentially use: a secret, a connection, or a piece of blackmail. The players may not find all of these, but having them ready gives you options when they get creative.
- A list of three NPCs the players care about who could be affected by the Audit's outcome. This creates stakes beyond the players' own survival.
- A complication from the Audit Complication table in Part Seven that you can deploy if the scene needs energy.
- The name and location of the Audit's aftermath scene. Where do the players go when the Audit is over? Who is waiting for them? What has changed while they were inside?
- A private note for each player that reveals something only their character would know about the Audit. This could be an offer from a faction, a warning from an ally, or a detail that only their character would notice. Slip these notes to them during the session.

**Three Things to Avoid When Running an Audit.**

One: do not make the outcome purely random. The dice have their place, but the players' actions during the Audit should matter more than the roll on the table. Adjust Risk based on what they do. A player who provides useful information to the investigator should feel their Risk decrease. A player who threatens an investigator should feel the consequences immediately.

Two: do not let the Audit become a solo scene for one player while everyone watches. If one player is being interrogated, cut to the others. What are they doing? Are they trying to bribe a clerk? Are they destroying evidence? Are they making deals with other officials who are also under investigation? The Audit should involve everyone, not just the person in the chair.

Three: do not forget the aftermath. The most important part of an Audit is what happens after it ends. The players have lost Celestial Favor, rank, and allies. They have made enemies. They have discovered who their real friends are. The session after an Audit should be about rebuilding, about the changed relationships, about the new dynamics. The Audit is a door. The aftermath is what lies on the other side.

## Part Five: The Twelve Figures as NPCs

### Bringing the Figures to Life

The twelve figures are not stat blocks. They are people with histories, wounds, desires, and secrets. This section provides guidance for portraying each one at the table.

### Cinnabar Heart

**Default Gender:** Male

**Voice:** Dry, precise, given to quoting bureaucratic regulations as a form of humor. He speaks as though every sentence is being recorded, because it is.

**Physical Tell:** He touches his temples when troubled. He has suffered the same recurring headache since he was thirty two.

**What He Wants From the Players:** Someone who asks genuine questions. He is surrounded by people who recite doctrine. He craves intellectual honesty the way a drowning man craves air.

**What He Fears:** That he will die before telling someone the truth about the forged edicts. That when he dies, the lie will become permanent.

**Scene Seed:** The players are summoned to his office. He serves them his private reserve tea, which means this meeting is not being recorded. He asks them a question that is clearly a test: "If you discovered that something the Court teaches is doctrine is... inaccurate, what would you do?"

_First Impression._ The players are shown into an office that is aggressively ordinary. Filing cabinets. A chipped tea set. A window that looks out on a brick wall. The Cinnabar Heart sits behind a desk covered in paperwork that appears to have been there for years. He is a thin man of sixty two with sallow skin and the exhausted eyes of someone who has not slept well since before the players were born. He does not stand to greet them. He gestures to chairs that are slightly lower than his own. The interview begins with a long silence while he finishes writing something. He does not look up. When he finally speaks, it is to ask a question that sounds bureaucratic but is not. "The Bureau of Harmonious Narrative's quarterly report cites a seven percent reduction in doctrinal deviation. Do you consider this a success or a failure?" There is a correct answer. There is also a true answer. He is listening for the difference.

_Sample Quotes for the Host._

1. "The regulation requires that all documents be filed in triplicate. Do you know why? Because one copy is for the record. One copy is for the Commission. And one copy disappears. The disappearing copy is the one that matters."
2. "I have served under seventeen Directors of the Bureau. Twelve were Disappeared. Three retired. Two are still alive, though I would not call what they are doing living. The secret is not to be valuable enough to destroy. The secret is to be tedious enough to ignore."
3. "You asked me a question earlier. You think I did not answer. I did answer. You simply did not recognize the answer because you were expecting a different form."
4. "This tea is from my private reserve. Drink it. It may be the last honest thing anyone serves you in this building."

_If the Players Ally With Him._ He is mercurial but effective. He provides information, access, and escape routes. He never lies to them, though he frequently omits crucial details. He expects the same treatment. A player who betrays his trust will find that their secrets have a way of becoming public at the worst possible moment. A player who proves trustworthy will find that the Shadow has been protecting them from threats they never knew existed.

_If the Players Oppose Him._ They will never succeed. He knows where they sleep. He knows who they love. He knows what they are afraid of. He does not use this knowledge to threaten them. He uses it to ensure that their plans fail in small, inexplicable ways. A document goes missing. A contact does not show up. A door that should be unlocked is locked. The Shadow does not make enemies. He makes obstacles.

_If the Players Ignore Him._ He continues his work. He may approach them later if they become useful. He may not. He has many projects and many interests. The players are one thread in a very large web. He will pull on them when he needs them. Until then, he watches.

_Additional Scene Seeds._

1. A player wakes to find a note pinned to their pillow. It is written in a script they do not recognize. It reads: "Your contact in the Bureau is compromised. Do not trust anything they tell you after today. You will know this is true when they fail to recognize you tomorrow." The note is unsigned. The player's door was locked from the inside.
2. The Shadow appears at a player's elbow during a crowded social event. He does not look at them. He speaks in a low murmur. "In approximately three minutes, a messenger will arrive with news that will upset you. When you react, watch the person standing by the east pillar. They will smile. That smile will tell you who has been working against you."

### Duke of Eternal Night

**Default Gender:** Male

**Voice:** Cultured, amused, faintly melancholy. He speaks in complete paragraphs. He references events that happened before anyone else in the room was born. He is lonely in a way that eight centuries of life makes inevitable.

**Physical Tell:** He does not breathe. Watch closely. His chest never moves. It is the only visible sign of what he is.

**What He Wants From the Players:** Novelty. He has seen everything. Please, show him something new. Surprise him. He will reward you extravagantly for the gift of not being bored.

**What He Fears:** That his bloodline will end. That eight centuries of accumulated wisdom will mean nothing. That the last thing he hears will be the sound of an empire he helped build collapsing into irrelevance.

**Scene Seed:** The players are invited to his estate for a private dinner. The food is exquisite. The wine is older than most countries. He is a gracious host. Midway through the meal, he sets down his glass and says: "I am going to tell you a story about the night the empire was founded. Whether you believe it is your affair. But I think you should hear it from someone who was there."

_First Impression._ The Duke of Eternal Night's estate is a museum of things that no longer exist. The architecture predates the current Court. The furniture is from a lost dynasty. The art on the walls depicts landscapes that have been swallowed by the Dusk. The Duke himself is a study in elegant decay. He is tall, impossibly thin, dressed in black silk that seems to absorb light. His skin is pale, not with illness but with the particular pallor of something that has not seen sunlight in a very long time. His movements are slow and deliberate, as though he is conserving a finite resource. His smile is genuine, warm, and deeply unsettling, because it is the smile of someone who has nothing to fear and nothing to prove. When he speaks, his voice carries the weight of centuries. He calls the players by their full names, having researched them before the invitation. He knows more about them than they know about themselves. He does not mention this.

_Sample Quotes for the Host._

1. "I was there when the vote was taken. I voted against the sealing. I lost, and then I failed to stop what followed. That is the tragedy of longevity, is it not? You must live not only with what you chose, but with what your courage failed to prevent."
2. "The food is safe. I cannot eat it myself, but my chef is the finest in the empire. I hire only the best. I have had eight centuries to learn what excellence looks like."
3. "You are wondering what I am. It is a reasonable question. I am not a demon. I am not a spirit. I entered the blood centuries before the Emperor was sealed, by choice, with the terms explained plainly. I have regretted many things. That choice is not among them."
4. "My family has served the empire since before the empire was an empire. We were here when the first foundation stone was laid. We will be here when the last stone falls. Assuming we do not fall first."
5. "The Celestial Book believes knowledge is power. She is wrong. Knowledge is the memory of power. True power is the willingness to act on what you know. I have acted. I regret much of it."

_What He Is Doing Right Now._ The Duke is pursuing three objectives for the years ahead. First, he is searching for a successor. The one person he brought into the blood as an heir attempted a coup and now lives in exile. He is looking for someone who can inherit his knowledge and responsibilities without repeating that failure. The players may be candidates. Second, he is monitoring the Spire of Eternal Vigil for signs of change. He knows the seal is weakening. He is calculating how much time remains before it fails. Third, he is maintaining a secret correspondence with the Celestial Book, the Guest Among Forests, and the Cinnabar Heart. These four share a secret: they know what was done to the Emperor. They do not agree on what should be done about it.

_If the Players Ally With Him._ He becomes a patron of extraordinary resources and knowledge. He provides access to lost history, ancient artifacts, and connections that span centuries. He asks for little in return: only that the players surprise him, challenge him, remind him that the world is still capable of novelty. His protection is absolute within his sphere of influence. Outside it, he cannot help. His power is real but bounded.

_If the Players Oppose Him._ He is not threatened by them. He has seen a thousand enemies rise and fall. He does not retaliate. He waits. He knows that time is on his side. A player who makes an enemy of the Duke will find that doors close, invitations stop arriving, and allies grow distant. The Duke does not destroy his enemies. He outlives them. He has been doing it for centuries.

_If the Players Ignore Him._ He watches with mild interest. He may invite them again in a year, or a decade. He is patient. He has time that the players cannot imagine. If they never return, he will remember them with the vague fondness one feels for a passing acquaintance.

_Additional Scene Seeds._

1. The Duke summons a player who has shown interest in the Court's history. He leads them to a locked room in his estate. Inside is a painting, covered in dust. The painting shows the founding of the Court. In the painting, standing at the Emperor's right hand, is a figure with the player's face. "You have been here before," the Duke says. "You do not remember. But you have."
2. A player receives a gift from the Duke: an ancient scroll case made of jade and silver. Inside is a single sheet of paper with a name written on it. The name is the player's own, written in a script that predates the current Court. On the back, in the Duke's handwriting: "I have been expecting you. I was expecting your ancestor. The name passes down. The debt passes with it."

### Iron Bone

**Default Gender:** Male

**Voice:** Two voices. The first is warm, passionate, the voice of a leader who genuinely loves his people. The second is a growl that vibrates in the chest, the voice of the wolf. He shifts between them without warning.

**Physical Tell:** His hands tremble when the wolf is close. He keeps them in his pockets or behind his back.

**What He Wants From the Players:** Companions. Not followers. Not admirers. People who will stand beside him when the barricades go up. He has too many followers already. Followers become a mob. True companions become a movement.

**What He Fears:** The wolf. He is terrified of what he becomes when the rage takes him. He fears that one day the wolf will emerge and he will not come back.

**Scene Seed:** The players encounter him in a worker's canteen in the Eastern Foundries, not as a leader but as someone who came to eat and listen. He buys them a meal. He asks about their lives before the Court. He genuinely wants to know. When someone at the next table mentions a recent factory death, his eyes flicker, and for a moment, something much older than him looks out through his pupils.

_First Impression._ The Iron Bone looks like a factory worker who has been promoted too many times. His hands are scarred. His face is lined. His clothes are simple and practical. He moves through the world like someone who expects to be ignored, which is useful because it means people underestimate him constantly. When he speaks in his first voice, it is warm, genuine, filled with the particular charisma of someone who actually believes what he is saying. He makes eye contact. He listens when others speak. He remembers names. But there is a second presence behind his eyes, something ancient and hungry that stirs when he is angry or passionate. The players can see it when he talks about the factory deaths. They can hear it when his voice drops. They sense that the Iron Bone is not one person. He is a door, and something lives behind the door.

_Sample Quotes for the Host._

1. "The Court tells you that the empire is eternal. Look around you. The walls are cracking. The children are hungry. The old ways are killing us. How eternal does that feel?"
2. "I have a voice inside me that wants to burn everything down. Every day I choose not to listen to it. Some days the choice is harder than others. Today is a hard day."
3. "You ask what I want. I want the foundries to be safe. I want the workers to be paid enough to feed their families. I want the children to grow up without the fear of the Commission. These are not radical demands. They are basic decency."
4. "The wolf is not my enemy. The wolf is my anger, given form. Anger is not evil. It is information. It tells you when something is wrong. The question is what you do with the information."
5. "The Golden Orthodoxy says the Dusk is a punishment for heresy. It did not begin in the camps. I know that. But I watched it enter one. The Court gathered suffering in one place until the red found a way through. Sin did not call it inward. Our cruelty did."

_What He Is Doing Right Now._ The Iron Bone is organizing on three fronts. First, he is building a clandestine network of worker councils across the capital's industrial districts. These councils are designed to coordinate action when the moment comes. They are not revolutionary cells. They are mutual aid societies that happen to have a political edge. Second, he is investigating the history of the penal works where the Dusk first appeared beyond the border provinces. He has found witnesses, survivors, and partial records. He is piecing together what happened. Third, he is managing the wolf. He meditates daily. He performs rituals of containment. He has had no public loss of control in seven years, though private memory gaps still frighten him. He is not sure how much longer he can maintain the public record.

_If the Players Ally With Him._ He welcomes them as equals, not followers. He shares everything he knows. He introduces them to his network. He asks for their skills, their ideas, their criticism. He is the least hierarchical leader in the Court. He genuinely believes that the movement belongs to everyone, not to him. He will follow the players' lead if they demonstrate better judgment. This is not humility. It is strategy. He knows he is a weapon. He wants to be aimed by people he trusts.

_If the Players Oppose Him._ He is hurt but not vengeful. He tries to understand their objections. He attempts to persuade them. If they become a threat, he does not attack them directly. He sends the wolf. He does this reluctantly, knowing that each time he unleashes the wolf, it becomes harder to cage it again. A player who opposes the Iron Bone may find themselves facing something that is not quite human.

_If the Players Ignore Him._ He continues his work. The movement grows with or without them. When the revolution comes, and he believes it will come, the players will have to choose a side. Ignoring him now does not mean the choice will not arrive later.

_Additional Scene Seeds._

1. The Iron Bone invites a player to a secret meeting in the basement of a condemned building. When they arrive, they find representatives from all six schools sitting together. They are not debating doctrine. They are planning a coordinated response to the Dusk's advance. The Iron Bone has done something the Grand Council could not do: he has brought the factions together. "The Court cannot save the empire," he says. "The people can. The people already are."
2. A player who has shown sympathy for the Common Flame receives a package. Inside is a worker's glove, stained with oil and blood. A note reads: "The hand that wore this glove belonged to a woman who died in the Eastern Foundries last week. The official report says it was an accident. I have evidence it was not. I need someone who can read the official reports without raising suspicion. Will you help?"

### Celestial Book

**Default Gender:** Female

**Voice:** Ancient, papery, given to long silences. When she speaks, it is often in quotations from texts no one else has ever read. Sometimes the quotations contradict each other. Sometimes they contradict themselves.

**Physical Tell:** She occasionally loses focus, as though listening to something no one else can hear. Then she snaps back with a remark so precise it makes you wonder if the distraction was real.

**What She Wants From the Players:** Curiosity. The genuine, hungry desire to understand. She has spent a thousand years accumulating knowledge. She wants to share it with someone who will appreciate it, not weaponize it.

**What She Fears:** That the knowledge will die with her. That no one is worthy to inherit the Archives. That the truth about the Emperor and the Dusk and the founding crime will be lost, and the empire will collapse not because it was destroyed but because it forgot what it was.

**Scene Seed:** A player who has shown genuine intellectual curiosity is granted access to the Imperial Archives. The Celestial Book is there, shelving scrolls. She seems frail. She seems confused. Then she hands the player a specific volume, open to a specific page, and says: "This is what you were looking for, I believe. You did not know you were looking for it. But you were. Read. I will be in the next aisle if you have questions."

_First Impression._ The Imperial Archives are overwhelming: endless shelves stretching into darkness, the smell of paper and dust and time. The Celestial Book is part of the architecture. She is small and stooped, dressed in robes that may once have been a specific color but have faded to something indeterminate. Her hair is white and thin. Her hands are spotted with age. She moves slowly, touching the shelves as she walks, as though drawing strength from the knowledge around her. When she speaks, her voice is papery, like something that has been read too many times. She does not look directly at the players at first. She looks past them, at something only she can see. Then, suddenly, she focuses. Her eyes are shockingly sharp. "Ah," she says. "You. I have been expecting you. I have been expecting you for a very long time. You are late." She smiles. It is not clear whether she is joking. She may not be sure herself.

_Sample Quotes for the Host._

1. "I have read every book in this archive. Every scroll. Every tablet. Every inscription. It took me four hundred years. I remember all of it. I forget nothing. It is not a blessing."
2. "The truth about the Emperor is written in three places. One is in this archive. One is in the Duke's estate. One is in a place that no longer exists. You must find the first two to learn where the third was."
3. "You ask if the Court can be saved. That is the wrong question. The correct question is: should it be saved? The Court is a machine for extracting value from human lives. Machines can be redesigned. They can also be dismantled."
4. "I knew your great grandmother. She asked better questions than you do. But you are younger. You have time to improve."
5. "The Crimson Dusk is not a punishment. It is not a natural disaster. It is a symptom. The first wound was struck at the founding. One hundred and fifty five years ago, the completed seal tore it open again. The Dusk is the fever that followed. Fevers can kill you. They can also cleanse you."

_What She Is Doing Right Now._ The Celestial Book is engaged in three monumental tasks. First, she is reorganizing the Archives according to a system only she understands, a system that encodes a hidden message about the Emperor's true fate. She is racing to complete this before she dies. Second, she is training a successor: a young archivist who does not know she is being trained. She believes she has the capacity to inherit the knowledge if she can survive the Court long enough. Third, she is slowly, methodically destroying the false records that the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative has planted. She replaces them with accurate copies that differ in specific, crucial details. She has been doing this for two centuries. No one has noticed.

_If the Players Ally With Her._ She becomes a source of unparalleled knowledge. She answers questions directly, though often elliptically. She provides documents, clues, and historical context. She does not offer protection or resources. She offers understanding. A player who earns her trust is given access to the restricted sections of the Archives, where the real history of the Court is preserved. She asks only that they use the knowledge wisely and share it with no one until the time is right.

_If the Players Oppose Her._ She does not fight them. She does not need to. She simply stops helping them. The information they need becomes just out of reach. The document they were looking for has been moved. The reference they needed has been misfiled. The passage that would have explained everything is in a book that is currently being restored. The Celestial Book does not make enemies. She simply becomes unavailable to them. The silence of the Archives is her weapon.

_If the Players Ignore Her._ She continues her work. She may reach out to them if their actions suggest they are ready for certain knowledge. She does not pursue them. She has waited a thousand years. She can wait a little longer for the right students to find her.

_Additional Scene Seeds._

1. A player who has been researching the Dusk finds a book that should not exist. It is a history of the Dusk written from the perspective of the Dusk itself. The author is listed as "Anonymous." The handwriting matches the Celestial Book's. The final page reads: "The Dusk is not unconscious. It is dreaming. It dreams of the world before the sealing. It wants to go back. It cannot. Neither can we."
2. The Celestial Book approaches a player in a corridor and presses a scroll into their hands. "Read this tonight. Tell no one. If anyone asks where you got it, say you found it in your quarters. If they press, say you do not remember where you found it. If they press harder, say nothing at all. Silence is safer than lies." The scroll contains a single page: a fragment of the original charter, listing the rights of the Emperor over the Court. The rights are absolute. The charter was amended three days after the Emperor was sealed.

### Bone Script

**Default Gender:** Male

**Voice:** Plain and slightly slow, because the capital dialect is his third language and he assembles each sentence before he releases it. He gets honorifics wrong and has stopped apologizing for it. When he is angry he becomes more precise rather than louder, which officials find far more disturbing than shouting.

**Physical Tell:** His hand goes to his belt and counts tokens without his eyes following. He does it when he is thinking, when he is frightened, and when someone says something he intends to remember. He also looks at the plants in a room before he looks at the people.

**What He Wants From the Players:** Accuracy. Not sympathy, not donations, not outrage on his behalf. He wants someone to say a name correctly and not need to be told twice. Everything else he can arrange himself.

**What He Fears:** That the four thousand and one will be made into a symbol. A symbol can be argued with, adopted, printed on a banner, and eventually retired. A name in a registry cannot. He has watched the Common Flame try to turn him into a cause, and he understands that the cause would outlive the names and replace them.

**Scene Seed:** The petitions hall, sixth hour, a queue of ninety people. A player waiting on unrelated business notices that the man three places ahead is not waiting like the others. He is not anxious. He has a folded cloth on his knees, a stack of forms squared at the corners, and the specific stillness of someone who has done this four hundred times. When the window closes for the day without reaching him, he stands, thanks the clerk by name, and leaves. He will be back before dawn.

_First Impression._ He does not look like a threat and he does not look like a beggar, which makes him hard to place, and being hard to place in the capital is itself a kind of danger. The cloak is good work gone shabby, lined with living moss that he waters. The staff is ash felled by a storm, with raw Qi crystals growing where the branches fork, unset and unpolished, which no licensed practitioner would tolerate. Forty one bone tokens hang along his belt and harness on waxed thread. He is perhaps twenty five. He looks older in the eyes and younger everywhere else. When he speaks to the players he takes a moment first, and the moment is not hesitation. It is translation.

_Sample Quotes for the Host._

1. "The record is not false. Every sentence in it is true. It says the population relocated voluntarily. They did relocate. Nobody wrote down what the alternative was, because writing that down was not required by the form."
2. "You say the past cannot be changed. I am not asking to change it. I am asking for it to be written down. Those are different requests, and only one of them is impossible."
3. "I have four hundred and six hours in that hall. Clerk Yuan has looked for the correct form for seven months. He has never once asked me for money. I do not know what to do with that."
4. "Your Bureau has a word for people like me. I know the word. I would rather you used my prefecture's name, but you cannot pronounce it, and I have decided not to hold that against you."
5. "It is not a grave. A grave has a location. This is a belt."

_What He Is Doing Right Now._ Three things. First, the petition: complete, valid, and stalled for want of one signature from an official of the fourth rank or higher. Second, unlicensed work in the camps outside the walls, finding water, reading foundations, sitting with the dying, paid in food and information. Third, and known to no one, he is deciding what to do about Nine Willow Bend, where there are cook fires burning inside a declared Dusk zone and people who went back are not dying. He understands precisely what entering that into any record would summon, and he has not yet decided whether the names are worth it.

_If the Players Ally With Him._ He is the most useful ally in the empire for anything that involves the parts of the map the Court has stopped maintaining. He knows the camps, the unregistered roads, the water, and which ruins are safe to sleep in. He will not lie for the players and he will not pretend an opinion he does not hold, which makes him a poor conspirator and an excellent witness. He gives more than he takes and does not appear to notice the imbalance.

_If the Players Oppose Him._ He does not retaliate, and this is worse than retaliation. He simply continues, and continuing is the entire threat he represents. Every week he stands in that hall, the number of officials who have seen him standing there goes up. Players who move against him will find that obstructing a man asking for a stamp is very difficult to explain to anyone watching, and by the time they realize how many people are watching, the explaining has already started.

_If the Players Ignore Him._ The Court is manufacturing him. Every refusal, every lost form, every clerk who suggests he try a different window is another month of a young man learning exactly how the machine is assembled from the inside of the queue. Ignored long enough, he stops petitioning. What he does instead is the campaign's problem.

_Additional Scene Seeds._

1. A player who has earned some trust is invited to watch him do the evening maintenance: recutting a worn stroke, replacing a cracked thread, oiling the bone. It takes an hour. He does not narrate it. Partway through he hands the player a token and a knife and says the name aloud, twice, waiting for the player to repeat it. Whether the player gets it right is not the test. Whether they try is.
2. The Common Flame stages a rally and puts his name on the banners without asking. He arrives, stands at the back, and does not speak. Afterward an organizer explains warmly that the movement can get his petition signed within a week. It is true. The price is that the four thousand and one become the movement's dead rather than his valley's dead. The players are present when he answers.

### Incense Crown

**Default Gender:** Female

**Voice:** Unfailingly gracious, which is a technique and not a warmth. She speaks slowly and never fills a silence. Officials trained on the Court's verbal fencing find themselves talking into her pauses and saying appreciably more than they intended, and then leaving convinced the conversation went well.

**Physical Tell:** The third strand of her necklace. She touches it when a question has landed somewhere it was not supposed to reach. She is aware of the tell. She has decided that a legible tell she controls is safer than a hidden one she does not.

**What She Wants From the Players:** To be addressed as a person rather than as an office. It has been eleven years. She has stopped expecting it, which is different from having stopped wanting it.

**What She Fears:** Being asked the Temple's ninth year teaching by someone she cannot refuse. She has structured her entire adult life around never being in that room. She also fears, more quietly, that the arrangement is not a cage at all and that she has simply been comfortable, in which case the eleven years were a choice.

**Scene Seed:** A Court ceremony. She is present, crowned, veiled, silent, and functionally furniture. The players watch three separate officials discuss sensitive matters within her hearing without lowering their voices, because a woman who has been decided to be an object does not require discretion. Later, in a corridor, she says one sentence to a player that could only be built from something she overheard, and she says it without any expression at all, and then she is gone.

_First Impression._ Everything visible about her was designed by people long dead to produce a specific effect on the nervous system of a frightened human being, and it works, and she is the only living person who knows exactly how it is built. Pierced silver and river jade, hung with drops tuned four centuries ago to be inaudible past three paces. Unbleached silk veil. Six strands at the throat, each a completed rite. White gown, which in the Nine Domains is mourning, embroidered in cinnabar and gold in a pattern older than the imperial script and never satisfactorily translated. She is younger than the players expect. She does not move like an object. She moves like someone conserving something.

_Sample Quotes for the Host._

1. "You may ask. I am permitted to be asked anything. I am not, unfortunately, permitted to be alone, which is a different constraint and the one that matters."
2. "My attendants report on me weekly. I taught them to read. I have thought about the contradiction and I have decided to keep both halves of it."
3. "The Bureau spent nine years reconstructing the ordination from documents. What they produced is liturgically perfect. It is also completely inert. They have never been able to explain why, and I have never offered."
4. "The empire can take a province. It cannot take a rite. That is the whole of my position, and I would ask you to notice that it is a position I did not choose and cannot resign."
5. "The Duke told me my predecessor put a rising tone on the eighth phrase and her teacher did not. He will not say which was correct. I have come to understand that he does not know either. Neither does anyone. We have been doing it both ways for a hundred and forty years and nothing has happened, and I would very much like to know what that means."

_What She Is Doing Right Now._ She is writing down the ordination. Not the liturgy, which exists, but the part that is transmitted only in person and that nine people alive still carry. She is doing it in fragments, in a script she invented, distributed across objects nobody would search, and she is racing the actuarial table: the other eight officiants are all eighty one or older. She is also, in the third hour of every afternoon while her attendants eat, running a sanctuary in the shrine of the drowned. It currently holds six people, has held as many as nineteen at once, and has sheltered more than two hundred people over eleven years.

_If the Players Ally With Her._ She offers what nothing else in the campaign offers: legitimate immunity. A rite she is willing to perform can stop a proceeding that armies could not, because to interrupt it is to interrupt the Emperor's continuance, and no official in the Nine Domains wants that entry in the record next to their name. She will spend this for people, not for causes, and never twice for the same person.

_If the Players Oppose Her._ She has no soldiers, no coin, and no faction. She has forty minutes a day of unobserved time, two attendants who love her more than they love the Commission, and an unbroken habit of asking procedural questions in rooms full of people who have to answer them. Players who move against her will not be attacked. They will find themselves being asked, publicly and courteously, to clarify a small matter of form, and then another, until whatever they were doing has become a matter of record.

_If the Players Ignore Her._ Nothing happens for a long time, which is the point of her. Then an officiant dies, and there are eight. Then seven. The Host should mention each death in one sentence, without comment, at intervals. When the players finally understand what the count is measuring, they should be able to look back and see that they were told.

_Additional Scene Seeds._

1. A player brings her someone who needs shelter and does not ask how the arrangement works. She takes the person, says nothing, and afterward gives the player a small object: a bead from the fourth strand. She does not explain it. It is not a favor owed. It is evidence, of a kind, that she chose to place in someone else's hands.
2. She asks a player to accompany her to the sealed door for the Continuance, because it is permitted to bring an attendant and she has never used the permission. She performs the rite. At the eighth phrase she does not use the rising tone. Afterward she asks the player, quite calmly, whether they noticed anything answer differently, and it becomes apparent that she has just used the players to run an experiment on the foundations of the empire.

### Stray Stroke, Who Is Not One of the Twelve

**Default Gender:** Male

Include this figure only when your table wants the Court's count questioned rather than expanded. He is not a thirteenth Figure. He is the demonstration that the Twelve are twelve because somebody decided to spend the money on twelve.

**Voice:** Easy, warm, and slightly amused, with the smoothed accent of a man who has spoken seven prefecture dialects and now speaks none of them cleanly. He answers questions directly and completely, which disarms officials trained to expect evasion. He never rushes, never raises his voice, and never wins an argument he could have lost more usefully.

**Physical Tell:** When he is telling the truth about himself he slows down and stumbles, because he has almost no practice. He has no tell at all when lying. Hosts should let the players work out which of those two facts is the frightening one.

**What He Wants From the Players:** A fifth attempt at a question he has failed four times, and eventually the standing to walk into the Imperial Archives and ask for one document without giving a name he does not believe.

**What He Fears:** Reading his own name and feeling nothing. He has considered this for two years and has arranged his life so that the moment keeps not arriving.

**Scene Seed:** A checkpoint. The players are being processed badly and slowly, and the traveler ahead of them in the line is being processed quickly, because his documents are squared at the corners and he has answered every question freely and asked the inspector a friendly question about the southern road. He is through the gate in ninety seconds. Later, on the road, they overtake him. He is in no hurry. He offers to share a fire and asks nothing about who they are.

_First Impression._ He does not look like anything. That is not a failure of description; it is the whole of the description. A road coat in the colors of wet ground, a wide hat, a long inscribed scarf that reads as decoration, boots with the heels worn unevenly. He is about forty. He is pleasant to everyone including the people the players are being rude to. Nothing about him invites a second look, and the players will realize an hour later that they cannot describe his face.

_Sample Quotes for the Host._

1. "I have never forged a document. Everything I carry was issued by an office of this empire and every word of it is true. I did not beat your system. Your system made all of these men. I stood there while it did."
2. "You are asking which name is the real one. None of them. That is not modesty and it is not a riddle. It is a fact about the paperwork."
3. "A verification query costs eleven copper to send and an afternoon of a clerk's life to chase. No office in the Nine Domains has ever been given a budget for suspicion. That is the entire secret. You may have it."
4. "I was thirteen and the tide stopped going out. Then there was a queue, and a clerk, and a book. I have been in that queue ever since, if you want the poetic version, which I do not recommend."
5. "I am going to lose this argument about the ferry price. Watch. He will remember a man who lost, and a man who lost is nobody, and being nobody in a way that makes a stranger cheerful is the finest work I do."

_What He Is Doing Right Now._ Three things. He is carrying, which is legitimate and pays badly. He is maintaining several hundred li of carrier marks that no one asked him to maintain. And he is standing outside the reading room of the Imperial Archives roughly once a month and not going in, because requesting a document requires stating who you are, and the only true answer he has is a sound he heard called across water when he was twelve.

_If the Players Ally With Him._ He is the best passage in the empire. He will move them, their people, and their documents between places the Court has stopped maintaining, and he will not ask what he is carrying, and he will not open it. He will not lie for them, because lying is a technique he retired, and he will not stay, because he has never stayed anywhere.

_If the Players Oppose Him._ There is nothing to seize, revoke, or file. What happens instead is that the road stops being available to them. Crossings become expensive, guides become unavailable, and the marks at the gateposts start saying something about them that they cannot read.

_If the Players Ignore Him._ He goes on. Eventually one of two things occurs: either somebody with rank notices that people are leaving Xiaoyuan without being recorded leaving, or he finally walks into that reading room. The Host should decide early which of these the campaign is heading toward, and then let it take a long time.

_Additional Scene Seeds._

1. A player asks him his name. He gives one. Three sessions later, in an entirely different province, an official addresses him by a different one, correctly and without hesitation, and does not appear to be mistaken.
2. Bone Script and Stray Stroke are in the same room, and Bone Script greets him by the borrowed name, carefully and correctly, the way he says every name. The players who know what that name is will have to decide whether to say anything. Whatever they decide is the scene.

## Part Five, Section Two: Running Each Archetype

### Cinnabar Heart (Alchemist)

**Core Fantasy.** The Cinnabar Heart player wants to feel clever. They reshape reality through ink and bureaucracy, finding loopholes that do not officially exist and rewriting events after the fact. Their power is intellectual: they know how the system works, and they use that knowledge to achieve what others think is impossible.

**What to Track.** Monitor the player's use of Transmutation of Truth carefully. Record what they changed and where the discrepancy still exists in the archival record. Track elixirs prepared and their remaining duration. Note who witnessed altered documents and might later notice inconsistencies. Keep a mental list of favors the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative owes them.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. A rival alchemist has also altered a document, and the two conflicting versions will cross paths in an upcoming review. The player must decide whether to expose the rival or let the contradiction stand.
2. The player's elixir ingredients are running low, and one crucial component is controlled by a faction that wants a favor the player is unwilling to give.
3. An official who witnessed a past event the player altered is being transferred to the player's bureau. The official remembers the event differently than the records now show.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not punish creative use of Transmutation of Truth by making every alteration immediately discovered. Let the player feel powerful. At the same time, do not let alterations go completely unnoticed forever. The buried truth surfaces at dramatic moments. Avoid treating the player as a passive document processor; their Bureau assignment should be a source of interesting choices, not tedious paperwork.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Cinnabar Heart figure is this archetype's ultimate mentor or rival. The player's former teacher who taught them everything about narrative shaping makes for a powerful NPC. The forged edicts are this archetype's natural story hook: the player discovers that the documents they have been creating are part of a larger conspiracy that implicates their own superiors.

**Spotlight Moment.** The Grand Council is about to vote on a measure that would destroy everything the players have built. The Cinnabar Heart player produces a document they have been carefully cultivating for months: a fabricated edict that predates the current debate and renders the vote procedurally impossible. The Council cannot proceed. The player's long game pays off in front of the entire Court.

### Night Warbler (Shadow Hand)

**Core Fantasy.** The Night Warbler player wants to feel dangerous, competent, and invisible. They want the satisfaction of a perfectly executed plan, the clean strike, the exit before anyone realizes anything happened. Their power comes from precision and patience, not brute force.

**What to Track.** Keep a list of the player's active infiltrations and what evidence they might have left behind. Track their contacts and safe houses. Note which factions have noticed their activities and whether they have connected the player to specific operations. Monitor how many people in the Court know the player's true capabilities.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. The player is hired for a job by one faction, but the target is being protected by another faction that the player has ties to. The contract must be fulfilled or declined, and either choice has consequences.
2. A previous target survived and has hired the Night Warbler NPC to hunt the player. The predator becomes the prey.
3. The player's Professional Courtesy reveals that a trusted ally is also a trained operative. The moment of recognition creates a tense standoff or an unexpected bond.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not negate the player's stealth abilities by making every location impossible to infiltrate. The Night Warbler should be able to go where others cannot. Conversely, do not let them operate with zero consequences. Evidence accumulates. Survivors talk. Security improves. Each mission should raise the stakes for the next.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Night Warbler figure is the player's shadowy counterpart, someone who walks the same path. The player's family history with the Commission, or a childhood friend who became an operative for a different faction, creates rich personal stakes. The investigation into Council member Wei's death (central to the Night Warbler NPC) can be inherited by the player as a personal mission.

**Spotlight Moment.** The players are trapped in a room with no obvious exit, enemies approaching from both sides. The Night Warbler player reveals they planted a rope, unlocked a window, or bribed a guard three sessions ago, just in case. "This way," they say. "Follow me. Stay close. Stay quiet." Their preparation saves the party.

### Guest Among Forests (Long Lived)

**Core Fantasy.** The Guest Among Forests player wants to feel ancient and wise, someone who has seen empires rise and fall. Their power comes from patience, perspective, and a deep connection to the natural world. They think in centuries, not days, and they know that most crises are temporary when viewed from a sufficient distance.

**What to Track.** Track the player's connections in the Verdant Path and among nonhuman peoples. Note promises made, favors owed, and debts owed to them. Monitor the state of their forest bonds and plant allies. Keep a timeline of events relative to their long lifespan, so you can draw on their character's personal history when relevant.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. A forest the player has a bond with is threatened by Dusk contamination or industrial logging. The player must choose between protecting the forest and pursuing their current mission.
2. Someone from the player's distant past reappears, carrying a message or a debt that has waited decades to be collected.
3. The player is asked to mediate a dispute between nonhuman peoples and a Court faction. Both sides trust the player, and the outcome will shape regional politics for years.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not treat the player's long lifespan as a source of omnipotent ancient knowledge. They know much, but their knowledge is centuries old and may be outdated. Avoid making every scene about how old the character is. Their age is context, not content. Use it sparingly for maximum impact.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Guest Among Forests figure is a kindred spirit, an elder who understands the player's burden. The player's former home in the Northern Expanse can be threatened by Court politics. A nonhuman friend or mentor who taught the player their ways can be endangered, forcing the player to move on a human timescale rather than their natural one.

**Spotlight Moment.** The Court is deadlocked, two factions at an impasse, violence imminent. The Guest Among Forests player steps forward and describes a similar crisis from three centuries ago, in another kingdom that no longer exists. They explain what happened then, what was learned, and what might be done differently now. The Council listens. The player's wisdom changes the course of the debate.

### Iron Calculation (Artificer)

**Core Fantasy.** The Iron Calculation player wants to be the smartest person in the room, the one who sees patterns others miss, the engineer who can fix anything if given enough time and resources. Their power is systemic: they understand how the empire actually runs, and that understanding is leverage.

**What to Track.** Track the player's active constructs and their remaining Qi reserves for maintenance. Keep notes on infrastructure they have influenced or redesigned. Monitor their Faction Standing with the Iron Calculation school specifically, as this is their natural power base. Record any debts owed to them by officials whose systems they have improved.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. A critical infrastructure failure threatens the capital. The player must diagnose the problem under time pressure while officials interfere with political demands.
2. The player's personal calculations predict a disaster that the official models deny. They must find evidence to support their findings before it is too late.
3. A rival engineer from a different school challenges the player's design, forcing a formal demonstration of their work before a judging panel.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not let every problem be solved by pointing out logical inefficiencies. The Court runs on emotion, tradition, and corruption as much as on logic. Avoid making technical solutions too easy; interesting engineering problems have tradeoffs, costs, and ethical dimensions.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Iron Calculation figure is a direct superior or rival, a master of the same arts. The player's family background in the factories or mines creates natural friction with aristocratic schools. A mentor who taught the player their craft may now be working on something ethically questionable, testing the player's loyalty.

**Spotlight Moment.** A barrier or seal is failing, and conventional methods cannot repair it in time. The Iron Calculation player unveils a device they have been building in secret, designed specifically for this moment. They explain how it works, what it costs, and what the risks are. Then they set it in motion. The device works. The day is saved by engineering.

### Iron Wall (Bastion)

**Core Fantasy.** The Iron Wall player wants to be the shield, the one who takes the hit so others do not have to, the person whose word is their bond and whose presence makes everyone safer. Their power is integrity: they stand firm so that others can act freely.

**What to Track.** Track the player's military contacts and Faction Standing with the Garrison. Note any oaths they have sworn and to whom. Keep a list of people they have sworn to protect. Monitor their current military assignment and reputation among soldiers and officials. Track how many times they have had to choose between duty and conscience.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. The player is ordered to enforce a law they consider unjust, or to arrest someone they know is innocent. Military discipline conflicts with personal honor.
2. A former comrade appears, now fighting for a different faction or the Dusk. The player must confront someone they once fought beside.
3. Operation Empty Throne is mentioned in their presence, and they must decide whether to investigate or report the mention to their superiors.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not sideline the Iron Wall in sessions focused on intrigue. Their honor and presence matter even in social scenes; a soldier who stands at attention and speaks plainly commands respect. Avoid making every conflict physical; the Iron Wall's true strength is their integrity, and tests of that integrity are more interesting than tests of their blade.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Iron Wall figure is a commanding officer whose respect the player craves or has already earned. The player's family legacy of military service creates expectations and burdens. A soldier the player once commanded now serves in a dangerous posting, and the player feels responsible for their safety.

**Spotlight Moment.** An innocent is about to be executed on false charges. The court is in order. The guards are loyal to the Commission. The Iron Wall player steps forward and stands between the executioner and the condemned. They do not draw their weapon. They say: "If this execution proceeds, it will be over my body. I have sworn to protect the innocent. My oath is older than this court." The room waits. Someone blinks first.

### Bright Mirror (Justicar)

**Core Fantasy.** The Bright Mirror player wants to be the investigator who cannot be fooled, the judge who cannot be corrupted, the light that exposes every shadow. Their power is disciplined judgment: they test every claim until they know what holds, then act on that conclusion without hesitation.

**What to Track.** Maintain a dossier of the player's active investigations, with clues found, witnesses interviewed, and leads remaining. Track their Truth Sight usage and what they have learned. Monitor their Faction Standing with Bright Mirror and which Commission officials have reason to fear or resent them. Note any past cases where the player made a call that later proved wrong.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. The player receives an anonymous tip about corruption in a school they respect. The evidence is convincing but the source is unknown. Investigate or ignore?
2. A witness the player was going to question is found dead. The player's investigation has made them a target.
3. The player discovers that a superior in the Commission is protecting a guilty official. The player must choose between loyalty to the Commission and loyalty to the truth.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not undermine the player's Truth Sight by making every NPC a perfect liar. Let them catch lies and feel powerful. But remember that truth is not always justice; a truthful statement can be misleading, and an honest person can be wrong. Avoid making the Bright Mirror player the party's moral police; let other players make their own choices about truth and deception.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Bright Mirror figure is this archetype's ultimate exemplar, someone to emulate or surpass. The player's family may have been harmed by the corruption they now fight. A past case where they made a wrong judgment and punished an innocent haunts them and can return as a plot point.

**Spotlight Moment.** The villain has woven an elaborate web of lies, alibis, and false evidence. Everyone believes them. The Bright Mirror player presents their case methodically: here is the discrepancy, here is the witness who saw the truth, here is the document that proves everything. They lay it out with perfect clarity. The villain's facade crumbles. Justice is done.

### Shadow (Night Walker)

**Core Fantasy.** The Shadow player wants to be the one who knows everything, who has a plan for every contingency, who can talk their way into or out of any situation. Their power is leverage: they have the right secret at the right moment, and everyone knows it.

**What to Track.** Keep a running list of secrets the player has acquired, noting who they have leverage over and what that leverage is worth. Track their safe houses, contacts, and escape routes. Monitor which factions are aware of the player's activities and what they want from the player. Know what the player did not tell the rest of the party.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. A secret the player thought was buried resurfaces, threatening someone they care about or revealing their own hidden past.
2. The player is offered a deal they cannot refuse by a faction that knows more about them than they realized.
3. An old contact from the Warrens arrives with urgent news but also brings dangerous attention from the authorities.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not let the Shadow's information network solve every problem. Information is power, but it must be verified, interpreted, and acted upon. Avoid making the Shadow feel like a spy who does not belong among "real" characters; their skills are essential to every infiltration, investigation, and social maneuver.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Shadow NPC is a reflection of what the player might become, for better or worse. The player's family in the Warrens or their past as a disgraced official's child creates emotional anchors. A former partner who betrayed them, or someone they once betrayed, can return with unfinished business.

**Spotlight Moment.** The players are cornered by an enemy who has them dead to rights. Weapons drawn, no escape. The Shadow player reaches into their sleeve and produces a single piece of paper. "Before you kill us, you should know that I have arranged for this document to be delivered to the Bright Mirror if I do not check in within the hour. It contains everything about your operation. Your suppliers. Your contacts. Your escape routes. So the question is not whether you will kill us. The question is whether you want to survive the week." The enemy's weapon wavers.

### Duke of Eternal Night (Blood Cultivator)

**Core Fantasy.** The Duke of Eternal Night player wants to feel like a force of nature, someone whose presence changes the dynamics of any room, someone who has seen it all and is genuinely interested in novelty. Their power is presence: they have lived for centuries, and that weight cannot be faked.

**What to Track.** Track the player's ancient debts and obligations, favors owed across centuries. Monitor their bloodline connections and Faction Standing with the Crimson Lineage. Note which factions seek their endorsement and what they are offering. Track their hunger levels and any consequences of feeding. Know which events from the Court's history the player personally witnessed.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. An ancient debt is called in by someone the player barely remembers, forcing them into an obligation they cannot refuse without losing face before the Crimson Lineage.
2. A rival blood cultivator from the player's past arrives in the capital, bringing old grudges and new threats that only the player understands.
3. The seal on the Spire weakens, and the player feels it in their blood. Something is changing, and the player is uniquely positioned to understand what.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not let the player's ancient power solve every problem through sheer authority. Their influence is real but bounded; the modern Court does not always bow to ancient names. Avoid portraying the player as disconnected from mortal concerns; their long life has given them deep appreciation for human moments, not contempt for them.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Duke of Eternal Night NPC is the player's sire, mentor, or rival, a figure whose shadow the player has lived in for centuries. The player's bloodline family members who do not carry the immortal burden create poignant relationships. A mortal lover or friend from centuries past, now reincarnated or descended, can create powerful emotional stakes.

**Spotlight Moment.** A crisis erupts in the Grand Council Chamber. Officials are shouting, factions are blaming each other, no one can agree. The Duke of Eternal Night player rises. They do not shout. They speak in the same calm, ancient voice they have used for centuries. "I was present at the founding of this Council. I have seen two hundred and forty three such crises. I have seen two hundred and forty two of them resolved. I will see this one resolved as well. Here is what we will do." And because of who they are, the Council listens.

### Iron Bone (Fury Adept)

**Core Fantasy.** The Iron Bone player wants to be the voice of the oppressed, the champion who fights for those who cannot fight for themselves, while wrestling with a destructive inner power. Their power is righteous anger: they have the wolf inside them, and the wolf does not negotiate with oppressors.

**What to Track.** Track the player's control over the wolf, noting what triggers them and how close they are to losing control. Monitor their Faction Standing with the Common Flame and the worker's movement. Keep a list of people they have promised to protect. Track their relationships with factory workers, laborers, and the poor. Know what the player would sacrifice to protect a stranger.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. A factory where the player has made connections is about to be shut down, throwing hundreds out of work. The owner is a faction ally of someone the party needs, forcing a hard choice.
2. Someone the player knows from their past life in the penal works or factories arrives at the capital, in danger and needing protection from the same forces that once imprisoned the player.
3. The wolf is triggered by an injustice the player witnesses, and they must make a Resolve check to maintain control in a situation where violence would be catastrophic to the party's goals.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not treat the wolf as a simple "Hulk out" button. The Iron Bone's struggle with their inner nature is central to their story; losing control should always have consequences, even when it is useful. Avoid making every session about the wolf; the player is also a skilled organizer and advocate, and those skills deserve spotlight too.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Iron Bone NPC is a cautionary tale, someone who has fully embraced the wolf and lost themselves. The player's family in the factories or their comrades from the penal works are powerful emotional anchors. A fellow organizer who looks up to the player can be endangered by the player's political enemies.

**Spotlight Moment.** The party faces an enemy that cannot be defeated by skill or strategy alone, a brute force problem that requires raw power. The Iron Bone player stops fighting the wolf. They let it rise. They describe the transformation: bones shifting, teeth lengthening, the world turning red. They turn to the Host and say: "I am going to protect my friends. Nothing else matters." They roll. The wolf is unleashed. The enemy falls.

### Celestial Book (Archivist)

**Core Fantasy.** The Celestial Book player wants to be the one who knows the answer, who has read the right book, who can draw on accumulated wisdom to solve problems that baffle others. Their power is knowledge: they hold the true history of the Court, and that history has teeth.

**What to Track.** Maintain a running list of what the player has researched and discovered. Track the secrets they hold and who would be harmed if those secrets were revealed. Monitor their access level in the Archives and which factions have noticed their research. Keep notes on the true history they are uncovering and how it contradicts official doctrine.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. The player discovers a text that contradicts something they believe about their own past. Their personal history may be built on a lie planted in the archives by a faction.
2. A faction learns that the player has been accessing restricted materials and demands to know what they have found, sending agents to intercept the player's research.
3. The Celestial Book NPC summons the player and asks for their help with a research problem, testing whether the player has learned enough to be trusted with deeper secrets.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not make the player's knowledge a simple "roll to win" button. Knowledge creates complications. Knowing the truth about the Emperor does not make the player powerful; it makes them a target. Avoid giving the player all the answers at once; discovery should be a process, and partial knowledge should lead to difficult choices.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The Celestial Book NPC is the player's mentor, a figure of awesome knowledge who may be grooming the player as a successor. The player's family might have been archivists before them, or their access to knowledge might have come at the cost of a family relationship. A rival scholar who competes for the same discoveries creates productive tension.

**Spotlight Moment.** The party faces a puzzle or mystery that seems unsolvable. Everyone is stuck. The Celestial Book player says: "I have read something about this." They describe a text they found three sessions ago, a fragment of a lost manuscript, a coded reference in a minor work. The information unlocks the solution. The player's diligent research pays off in a moment of pure intellectual triumph.

### Bone Script (Hedgewarden)

**Core Fantasy.** The Bone Script player wants to be the one person at the table who is not inside the machine. No rank, no seal, no file. Their power is that the ordinary levers of Court pressure do not reach them, and their vulnerability is that the ordinary protections do not either. They are carrying something the empire has decided does not exist, and they intend to make it exist on paper.

**What to Track.** Track the petition: which office it is with, which official has touched it, how many hours the character has spent waiting. Track the tokens, by name, and note every time a name is spoken aloud correctly by anyone. Track who in the camps depends on them. Track which factions have noticed them and what each faction has decided they are useful for.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. The petition finally reaches an official with the authority to sign, and that official wants something in exchange that would compromise exactly the thing the petition is for.
2. Someone offers to have the four thousand and one names entered immediately, as a group, under a single collective designation. It is faster. It is legal. It erases the names in the act of recording them.
3. A survivor of the character's prefecture turns up in the capital, alive, and is not on the bone. The character has to decide what a completed archive means now.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not let the character become the party's designated conscience, delivering moral verdicts on everyone else's choices. He is not above the table; he is beneath it, and he knows things the others do not because of where he has been standing. Also resist making his grief the only thing about him. He is funny, oddly practical, and better at bureaucratic procedure than anyone expects, because he has had four hundred hours to study it.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** Clerk Sixth Rank Yuan is the relationship that matters most, and it should be underplayed: a tired man at a window who has spent seven months looking for the correct form and has never asked for anything. Grandmother Stone Voice, past ninety and still in the high forest, is the character's teacher and the only person who can tell him he is wrong. The Celestial Book said his prefecture's name correctly once, in a corridor, and then vanished, and the character has been unable to find her since.

**Spotlight Moment.** The party needs something the empire's records cannot provide: a road that was decommissioned, a well that was filled in, the real boundary of a district that no longer has a name. Everyone's research fails. The Bone Script player says: "I have been there." Not read about it. Been there. What follows is not a skill check. It is testimony, and for one scene the character is the only authoritative source in the room.

### Incense Crown (Oracle)

**Core Fantasy.** The Incense Crown player wants to hold the one thing the empire cannot seize, copy, or replace, and to discover that holding it is indistinguishable from being held by it. Their power is legitimacy: they can stop a proceeding that armies could not. Their cost is that they are never, at any hour of any day, alone.

**What to Track.** Track observation: who is watching, who is reporting, and what her two attendants have filed this week. Track the count of living ordained officiants, which begins at nine and only ever goes down. Track every use of her ritual authority, because each use spends credibility that does not regenerate. Track who knows about the sanctuary in the shrine of the drowned.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. An attendant is offered a promotion in exchange for a report the character cannot afford to have filed, and the attendant comes to the character first, which is itself a problem.
2. A faction requires her to declare publicly for a side. Declaring destroys her usefulness to everyone including herself. Refusing indefinitely is not available.
3. One of the eight remaining officiants dies. The character has not finished writing down the ordination, and she now has to decide whether to approach one of the survivors, all of whom have reasons not to trust her proximity to the Court.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not run her as a passive prisoner waiting for rescue. She has been managing her captors expertly for eleven years and is the most politically sophisticated person at most tables. Also do not let the ritual authority become a get out of jail card; every use is public, remembered, and slowly converts her from an untouchable necessity into an active political problem that somebody will eventually decide to solve.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** White Reed and Little Pine, aged twenty two and seventeen, inform on her weekly and she has taught them both to read. She feeds Little Pine harmless truths so the girl does not have to invent harmful ones, which means she has made herself responsible for the welfare of her own surveillance. The Bright Mirror has held a standing order to close her Temple for thirteen years and has not executed it, and in eleven meetings neither of them has said anything that could be written down.

**Spotlight Moment.** A proceeding is going badly. An ally is about to be Disappeared, the evidence is fabricated, the outcome is arranged, and there is no argument left that anyone in the room is obliged to hear. The Incense Crown player stands and states that the officiant of the Continuance requires the proceeding suspended pending a question of rite. It is a procedural nothing. It is also unanswerable, because the only official who could overrule her would be entering a refusal of the Emperor's continuance into the permanent record under his own name. The room stops. Nobody in it is happy. It stops anyway.

### Stray Stroke (Drifter)

**Core Fantasy.** The Stray Stroke player wants to be the one person at the table that the record cannot resolve. Their power is that every lever the Court reaches for closes on nothing. Their cost is that they have been eleven men and are no longer certain there is a twelfth underneath.

**What to Track.** Track the eleven names: which office issued each one, which is currently in use, and which have been spoken aloud in front of whom. Track every scene in which a name gets attached to the character by someone who believes it. Track the Amber Shore registry: how close the character has come to requesting it, and what stopped them. Track who has begun to suspect that two of the names are the same person.

**Scene Hooks.**

1. Two of the character's identities are required to be in the same room at the same time, by two separate parties who both have reason to expect a specific man.
2. Somebody offers, as a genuine kindness, to make one of the eleven permanent and official, with a family and a standing and a future. Refusing requires explaining why, and explaining why requires the truth.
3. A retired identity turns out to be current: taxes paid, address occupied, a different hand on the renewal form. Someone else has the method.

**Common Pitfalls.** Do not run this as a heist character with a wardrobe. Nothing is being stolen and nothing is forged; the papers are all real, and the horror is that they are all real. Also resist making him untouchable. Nothing to Take cuts both ways, and the Host should use the second half of it: no faction protects him, no patron's favor can be recorded on his behalf, and a man with no entry can be removed without one.

**Making Personal Connections Matter.** The dead net mender in Amber Shore who knew inside a month and fed him for four years is the emotional floor of the character, and it should surface rarely and land hard. Copper Ladle, the living man whose name he took, is the open wound and the reason he will accept a job that gains him nothing. Bone Script, who says the borrowed name correctly and with care, is the ongoing quiet cost of the whole arrangement.

**Spotlight Moment.** The party is cornered by procedure rather than violence: a checkpoint, a summons, an inspection, a list. Every other character has a rank to invoke or a faction to call, and every one of those threads is being watched. The Stray Stroke player asks the Host which prefecture issued the inspector's authority, produces a document from an oilcloth wrap, and answers four questions honestly. It takes ninety seconds and no roll is dramatic. Afterward the table sits with the fact that this was never a trick, and that the empire is held together by paper and by nobody having the budget to check.

## Quick Start Scenes

These twelve scenes are ready to drop into any session. Each is designed to run in five minutes of table time, providing a spark when the story needs one. Use them when the players are stuck, when you need a complication, or when you want to remind everyone that the Court is alive and dangerous.

### Scene One: The Wrong Door

**Hook.** The players open a door they should not have and find something that changes their course.

**Where It Takes Place.** A narrow corridor in the Bureau of Harmonious Records, third floor, west wing. The door is unmarked, indistinguishable from a dozen others. "The corridor narrows here. The Qi lamps on the walls have burned out, leaving only the faint blue glow from a crack beneath a door at the end. The dust on the floor is undisturbed. No one has walked this hallway in a very long time. The door is slightly ajar. You see a sliver of light inside."

**NPC.** A night clerk named Huan, a young man with fingers stained by ink and circles under his eyes. He is in the room, startled by the players' entrance. He has a stack of documents he is clearly not supposed to have. "Please. You were not supposed to be here. I was not supposed to be here. If you report me, I am dead. If I do not complete this work, I am also dead. I do not know which death comes faster."

**Complication.** Huan is forging travel permits for refugees trying to flee the Dusk. He is breaking the law for a good reason. If the players report him, he is Disappeared. If they help him, they become accessories to forgery. If they walk away, someone else will find him before the night is over.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Cinnabar Heart recognizes the forgery technique as one he uses himself. A Bright Mirror detects the lie in Huan's voice when he says he is working alone. An Iron Bone feels the wolf stir at the injustice of a man being punished for saving lives.

**Possible Outcome.** The players help Huan finish the permits and escape before the night patrol arrives. They gain a grateful contact in the lower bureaucracy and a stack of blank permits they could use themselves. Or they report him and gain Faction Standing with Bright Mirror, but lose the trust of everyone who hears about it.

### Scene Two: The Debt Collector

**Hook.** An old woman appears at the players' residence. She is not who she seems.

**Where It Takes Place.** The players' shared residence in the Outer Court. A modest building with paper walls and a small courtyard. Evening. "The light through the paper screens has turned amber. You hear the evening bell from the Ministry tower. Then you hear footsteps in the courtyard. A knock at the door. Not urgent. Patient. You open it to find an old woman in grey robes, leaning on a walking stick. She smiles. Her teeth are uneven. She says your name, and one of you, just one of you, recognizes her."

**NPC.** The old woman is a messenger from a debt that one player's family incurred generations ago. She carries a small wooden box. "Your grandfather borrowed something from my grandmother. It is time to repay. Do not worry. I do not want money. I want you to deliver this box to a location I will describe. Do not open it. Do not ask what is inside. Simply deliver it, and the debt is settled."

**Complication.** The box contains a Dusk Touched object. Delivering it will spread Dusk contamination to the location. Not delivering it means the debt collector will take something else: a memory, a name, a year of life. The player must choose which price to pay.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Celestial Book recognizes the box's markings as originating from a dynasty that predates the Court. A Duke of Eternal Night recognizes the debt collector's family name and knows they always collect. A Guest Among Forests senses the faint vibration of something alive inside the box.

**Possible Outcome.** The player delivers the box and a new Dusk pocket forms in the heart of the capital. Or the player refuses and loses an important personal resource, but gains the debt collector's grudging respect and a warning: "The Court will ask you to do worse. Remember that you had a choice today."

### Scene Three: The Execution Crowd

**Hook.** The players pass through a square where a public execution is about to take place.

**Where It Takes Place.** The Plaza of Manifest Virtue, a wide stone square in the Inner Court. Midday. "The crowd is thick. Hundreds of officials, merchants, and onlookers have gathered. In the center of the plaza, a wooden platform has been erected. On the platform kneels a man in prisoner's grey. His hands are bound behind him. A Commission herald reads the charges in a voice trained to carry: corruption, heretical association, conspiracy against the Grand Council. The condemned man does not look up. He seems almost bored. To your shock, you recognize him. He is the clerk who served you tea at the Ministry last week."

**NPC.** The condemned man's name is Ren. He is not a criminal. He is a scapegoat. A faction needed someone to take the fall for a failed operation, and Ren was the most expendable person in the building. He knows this. He accepted the deal because they promised his family would be protected. They have not kept that promise.

**Complication.** The execution is public. Intervening means exposing yourself before the entire Court. The Commission guards are numerous. The crowd is watching. But Ren's wife is in the crowd, and she is looking at the players with desperate hope. She knows they know the truth.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Night Warbler can spot three Commission agents in the crowd who are watching for anyone who reacts too strongly. A Bright Mirror can see that the Commission herald's hands are shaking as he reads the charges. An Iron Wall recognizes the executioner as a former soldier who was discharged for refusing an order.

**Possible Outcome.** The players do nothing and gain nothing but the memory of Ren's wife's face. Or they cause a distraction that lets Ren escape, making a powerful enemy in the faction that wanted him dead. Or they produce evidence of his innocence at the last moment, turning the execution into a political crisis.

### Scene Four: The Tea That Tells

**Hook.** A routine tea meeting becomes a confession.

**Where It Takes Place.** A small teahouse in the Outer Market, known for its cheap tea and its thick walls. Rain spatters against the paper windows. "The teahouse is nearly empty. A lone merchant reads a newspaper in the corner. The proprietress waves you to a table near the back, where the light is dim and the voices do not carry. You ordered tea. What arrived is a person. She sits down across from you without asking. Her robes mark her as a functionary of middling rank. Her knuckles are white around a cup she has not drunk from. She speaks without preamble. 'I know who arranged Council member Wei's death. I have known for three months. I have been too afraid to speak. I am still afraid. But I am more afraid of what will happen if I stay silent.'"

**NPC.** Her name is Lijuan. She is a records keeper in the Commission for Celestial Purity. While filing a report after hours, she saw a sealed authorization enter the liaison office used by the Bureau of Internal Harmony. She recognized the private seal, the routing marks, and the Council clerk who delivered it. She cannot identify the operative who carried out the killing. She can identify the office that arranged it. She has told no one. She has been waiting for someone who could do something about it.

**Complication.** Lijuan does not want the players to act on this information immediately. She wants them to confirm it first. She gives them a piece of evidence: a copied fragment of the routing slip attached to the authorization. Its paper and registration code were available to only three offices in the capital. One belongs to a member of the Grand Council.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Cinnabar Heart recognizes that the registration code was retired months ago. The evidence was planted. A Shadow knows that Lijuan is being watched and has been followed to this meeting. A Celestial Book has read a similar account in an old file: someone else witnessed a Council member's death and disappeared before they could testify.

**Possible Outcome.** The players follow the evidence and uncover a conspiracy within the Commission itself. Or they confront the Grand Council member directly, forcing a political crisis. Or they tell Lijuan she is being used, and she flees the capital, sending them a coded message from the road.

### Scene Five: The Locked Room

**Hook.** An official is found dead in a room locked from the inside.

**Where It Takes Place.** A private study on the fourth floor of the Ministry of Rites. The door is solid oak, bolted from within. "The crowd in the corridor parts for you. The guards have broken down the door, but they found the bolt still thrown. Someone locked this room from inside before they died. The study is small, cluttered with scrolls and unfinished calligraphy. The body is slumped over the desk, a brush still in the hand. The cause of death is not obvious. No blood. No wounds. The face is calm, as though the official simply stopped living in the middle of a sentence. The window is sealed with wax. The walls are solid. There is no other exit. You are looking at an impossible death."

**NPC.** The investigating guard captain is named Sergeant Deng. She is competent, tired, and suspicious of everyone. She does not trust the players but she needs their help because the dead official was connected to their bureau. "I have sealed the room. No one enters or leaves until we understand what happened. If you can tell me how this was done, I will owe you. If you cannot, I will have to report that I found you here, which is already an irregularity."

**Complication.** The death was caused by a Qi Technique that stops the heart. The technique is rare and only known to a specific school. The dead official was investigating that school's financial records. The killer is likely still in the building.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** An Iron Calculation notices that the ink in the brush is still wet, but the body is cold. The death occurred after the writing stopped, not during. A Bright Mirror sees a faint residue on the official's neck, invisible to the naked eye, a trace of the killer's Qi. A Night Warbler finds a hairline crack in the window seal, invisible from the corridor, a possible entry for a thin wire or blade.

**Possible Outcome.** The players identify the killer as a senior accountant in the school the victim was investigating. The accountant admits the murder but claims he acted to defend himself because the dead official was about to destroy evidence of a larger conspiracy. The truth is now in the players' hands.

### Scene Six: The Fugitive Scholar

**Hook.** A disgraced scholar begs the players for shelter, claiming to have discovered something the Court wants buried.

**Where It Takes Place.** The scholars' quarter, a quiet district of bookshops and scriptoria. Night. "The street is empty. The lanterns have been extinguished. The only light comes from a single window on the second floor of a bookbindery. That is where you find him: a man in his sixties, wearing the stained robes of someone who has been sleeping rough. His hands are covered in paper cuts and ink stains. He has been destroying documents. Not valuable ones. Just small pieces, things he could carry. He looks up when you enter and says your name. He has been waiting for you."

**NPC.** Scholar Heng was a respected historian until his last publication, which questioned the official timeline of the Court's founding. He was stripped of his rank and his access to the Archives. He has been in hiding for eight months. He has found something in the private collections of a sympathetic patron. "The Court's founding documents are not originals. They are copies. The originals were destroyed a year after the Emperor was sealed. Someone wanted to erase what the founding truly required. I have found a fragment of the original. It describes a ritual. A human sacrifice. The Emperor was not sealed. He was bound. There is a difference."

**Complication.** The Commission knows Scholar Heng is in the capital. They are searching for him. He has three hours before they narrow the search to this district. He needs the players to get him out of the city. He will not leave without his research, which fills two heavy satchels.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Celestial Book has read Scholar Heng's earlier work and knows he is meticulous but prone to overinterpretation. His discovery may be real or may be wishful thinking. An Iron Bone recognizes the scholar's description of the ritual from folklore the Common Flame has preserved. A Duke of Eternal Night knows the scholar's patron and warns that the patron is unreliable.

**Possible Outcome.** The players smuggle Scholar Heng out of the capital. He establishes a hidden study in a border town and sends them occasional letters with new discoveries. Or he is captured, and his research falls into the Commission's hands. Or the players read his work and learn a truth that changes their understanding of the Emperor's fate.

### Scene Seven: The Festival Interruption

**Hook.** A celebration is interrupted by a mysterious message.

**Where It Takes Place.** The Plaza of Celestial Harmony, decorated for the Festival of Unbroken Light. Lanterns in every color hang from wires stretched between buildings. Musicians play on a raised platform. "The plaza is alive with color and noise. Thousands of paper lanterns cast a warm glow over the crowd. Children run between the legs of adults. Food vendors call out prices. A troupe of actors performs a comedic skit about a foolish official who tried to outsmart the Iron Calculation. The crowd roars with laughter. Then the lanterns go out. All at once. The plaza plunges into darkness. For a single heartbeat, there is silence. Then the screams begin."

**NPC.** A performer from the acting troupe, a woman named Rulan, is found holding a piece of paper that appeared on the stage during the blackout. She hands it to the players with trembling hands. The message reads: "The Dusk is not at the border. It is here. Look up." When the players look up, they see that the red glow from the Spire of Eternal Vigil is brighter than it has ever been.

**Complication.** The blackout was not an accident. Someone sabotaged the Qi lamps. The saboteur is still in the crowd. The panic is spreading. If the players do not restore order, people will be trampled. If they chase the saboteur, people will be hurt in their absence.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Shadow recognizes the sabotage technique: it requires access to the Qi grid that only three people in the capital have. An Iron Wall can see the crowd's panic forming predictable patterns and can direct people to safety through the most efficient routes. A Guest Among Forests notices that the plants in the plaza's central garden are wilting, a sign that the Dusk's influence is physically present.

**Possible Outcome.** The players restore order and capture the saboteur, who is a member of a doomsday cult that believes the Dusk is divine judgment. From the saboteur's confession, they learn that the cult has agents throughout the capital and plans to accelerate the Dusk's advance through ritual vandalism of Qi infrastructure.

### Scene Eight: The Letter That Was Not Sent

**Hook.** A letter meant for someone else arrives in the players' hands.

**Where It Takes Place.** The players' assigned worktable in the Bureau of Correspondence. A long room filled with desks, each piled with documents. "The morning post has arrived. Your stack is larger than usual. You sort through permits, requests, and notifications. Near the bottom, a letter sealed with gold wax catches your eye. It is addressed to you, but the handwriting is unfamiliar. The seal is from the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative, but it is not the standard seal. It is a personal seal. Someone used their private chop on official correspondence. That is unusual. That is deliberate."

**NPC.** The letter is from an editor of middle rank in the Bureau named Chen. He wrote it in a panic and addressed it to the wrong person. The letter confesses that he has been ordered to rewrite the records of a recent Audit, removing the names of three officials who were Sanctioned and replacing them with three different names. He does not know why. He is afraid. He wrote to the one person he thought might help.

**Complication.** The three officials whose names were removed are alive and well. The three whose names were inserted have already been arrested and are awaiting transportation to a penal works. The switch was made to protect the original three, who are connected to a powerful faction. Chen is now a liability. The faction that ordered the switch will want to silence him.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Cinnabar Heart sees the handwriting pattern of the letter and recognizes it as a forgery. Chen did not write this. Someone wants the players to discover this discrepancy. A Bright Mirror can detect that the personal seal on the letter is not Chen's. It belongs to someone in the Commission who is using Chen as bait. An Iron Calculation compares the dates and finds that the original three officials have already left the capital. They are not in danger. They are already gone.

**Possible Outcome.** The players confront Chen (or his impersonator) and uncover a network of officials who have been falsifying Audit records for years. The network is bipartisan, drawing members from every school except the Bright Mirror. The players must decide: expose the network and trigger a political earthquake, or join it and gain a powerful shield against future Audits.

### Scene Nine: The Body in the Garden

**Hook.** A dead body is found in the Imperial Gardens, and the players are the first to arrive.

**Where It Takes Place.** The Imperial Gardens, a vast cultivated landscape of ponds, bridges, and precisely arranged trees. Dawn. "The mist rises from the koi pond. The air is cold and carries the scent of wet earth and flowering plum. The garden is empty at this hour, which is why you came here, to think, to breathe, to be away from the endless corridors of the Court. You find the body near the oldest plum tree, the one the Guest Among Forests planted herself. A woman in official robes lies on her back among the fallen petals. Her eyes are open. She is wearing a necklace of black jade that you recognize. It belongs to the Bright Mirror."

**NPC.** The dead woman is a Commission agent named Fuyan. She was the Bright Mirror's personal assistant. She has been stabbed with a blade that is still in the wound. The blade is a standard issue Commission dagger. The Bright Mirror's own weapon.

**Complication.** The Bright Mirror was seen entering the gardens last night. He was seen leaving, alone, at midnight. The dagger is his. The evidence points to him. But the Bright Mirror is the most incorruptible figure in the Court. If the players report this, they set in motion a crisis that could destroy the Commission. If they conceal it, they become accessories to a potential murder.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Night Warbler notices that the body was moved after death. The blood pattern is wrong. Fuyan was killed elsewhere and brought here. A Cinnabar Heart finds a single sheet of paper in Fuyan's sleeve, a note that reads: "I found what he is hiding. It is worse than we thought. Meet me at the old plum tree." A Guest Among Forests touches the tree and senses that it witnessed the arrival, not the killing. The tree's memory is of a body being placed, not a life ending.

**Possible Outcome.** The players investigate and discover that Fuyan had discovered the Bright Mirror's secret investigation into the forged edicts. Fuyan was killed by a third party who wanted to frame the Bright Mirror and stop his investigation. The real killer is a senior figure in the Golden Orthodoxy who was about to be exposed.

### Scene Ten: The Offer You Cannot Refuse

**Hook.** A faction makes the players an offer that is generous, dangerous, and impossible to decline politely.

**Where It Takes Place.** A private dining room in the Golden Orthodoxy's compound. Silk hangings. Low tables. The scent of expensive incense. "The dinner was unexpected. The invitation arrived on paper edged in gold, sealed with the Golden Orthodoxy's personal chop. The messenger waited for your reply. Dinner is at the eighth hour. Attendance is mandatory. Not by law. By implication. You arrive to find a room of severe elegance. Black lacquer furniture. A single calligraphy scroll on the wall, bearing a quote from the founding doctrine. Your host enters. He is a Minister of the Inner Circle, a man who has never spoken to anyone below the rank of Bureau Director. He is speaking to you. This is an honor. It is also a message."

**NPC.** Minister Feng is a man in his seventies, immaculately dressed, with the calm confidence of someone who has never been contradicted in public. He serves the players tea himself, a gesture of extreme respect that puts them in his debt. "I have been watching your work. You are competent. You are discreet. You have principles, which is rare and valuable. I want to offer you a position in the Golden Orthodoxy's inner secretariat. The pay is five times your current stipend. The protection is absolute. The work is simple: you will review documents for doctrinal consistency and flag anything that deviates from the founding principles. In return, the Golden Orthodoxy will be your shield, your patron, and your family."

**Complication.** The simple work is censorship. The Golden Orthodoxy wants to use the players to identify and suppress any documents that contradict their preferred narrative. The position comes with enormous power and enormous moral compromise. If the players refuse, Minister Feng will not threaten them. He will simply remember. And his memory is very long.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Celestial Book recognizes the calligraphy scroll as a forgery of a famous work. The quote on the wall is not from the founding doctrine. It is from a later addition that was never formally adopted. The Golden Orthodoxy is testing whether the players will notice. A Shadow has heard rumors that three previous occupants of this position have all died unexpectedly. The position may not be as safe as it sounds. A Bright Mirror notices that Minister Feng's hands are perfectly still, unnaturally still, as though he is holding himself in check. He is afraid of something. He is not the one in control here.

**Possible Outcome.** The players accept the position and gain a powerful patron, but find themselves complicit in the Golden Orthodoxy's censorship apparatus. They must balance their conscience against their safety. Or they refuse gracefully, making an enemy of the Minister but preserving their integrity. Or they refuse rudely, triggering immediate consequences and a very interesting session.

### Scene Eleven: The Night Market Informant

**Hook.** A contact in the Outer Market has information the players need, but they are not the only ones looking for them.

**Where It Takes Place.** The Night Market, a section of the Outer Market that comes alive after midnight. Lanterns in deep red and black. "The Night Market is a different world. The stalls are smaller, the goods stranger, the customers quieter. People here do not use their real names. The merchant you are looking for runs a stall that sells nothing: empty shelves, a single chair, a lamp. He sits in the chair and watches the crowd. He will not speak to you until you name the password. The password changes every night. You received it three hours ago from a messenger you have never met and will never see again."

**NPC.** The informant calls himself Empty Lantern. He is a thin man in his forties with a nervous cough and eyes that never stop moving. He sells information to anyone who can pay, but he is loyal to no one. "I have what you asked for. But there is a problem. Someone else asked for the same information. They paid more than you did. I gave them the same answer I am about to give you. Which means they know what you know. And they know you were coming here."

**Complication.** The other buyer has posted watchers around the Night Market. The players are being watched right now. If they leave the market openly, they will be followed. If they try to confront the watchers, they start a fight in neutral territory. Empty Lantern offers a solution: a tunnel exit beneath his stall that leads to a neighboring district. The tunnel is dark, narrow, and not entirely safe. But it is an exit.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Night Warbler can identify the lead watcher as a former colleague from a previous assignment. A Shadow has used this tunnel before and knows it opens beneath one of his safe houses. A Duke of Eternal Night recognizes the surveillance technique as one used by the Crimson Lineage's intelligence arm.

**Possible Outcome.** The players use the tunnel and escape. They have the information, but their enemies know they have it. The timeline has just shortened. Or they confront the watchers directly, turning the Night Market into a scene of chaos, and gain a reputation as people who do not back down.

### Scene Twelve: The Archivist's Request

**Hook.** A dying archivist asks the players to complete a task she cannot finish.

**Where It Takes Place.** The infirmary of the Bureau of Harmonious Records, a quiet room with white walls and the smell of medicinal herbs. "The woman in the bed is barely visible beneath the blankets. Her face is sunken. Her hands, resting on the coverlet, are covered in ink stains that will never fade. She is younger than you expected. The disease that is killing her has aged her prematurely, but her eyes are still sharp. She recognizes you. She has been waiting for you. Her voice is a whisper that costs her visible effort. 'I have something to give you. A key. A real key, not a metaphor. It opens a lock in the subbasement that no one has opened since the Alchemist era. I was supposed to open it before I died. I cannot. You must.'"

**NPC.** Archivist Suling has worked in the Ministry for forty years. She knows the location of every document, every hidden compartment, every forgotten room. She is dying of a lung disease contracted from decades of breathing archive dust. She has no family. Her life's work has been preserving knowledge that the Court wanted destroyed. "The room behind that lock contains the personal correspondence of the first Alchemist. He wrote letters to someone outside the Court. Someone who was not human. The correspondence continued after the Emperor was sealed. Someone on the outside wrote back. I do not know who. I could not read the final letter. It is in a language I do not recognize. You will need someone who can read what should not be readable."

**Complication.** The key activates a silent alarm in the Commission's monitoring office. The players have approximately fifteen minutes from the moment they use it before a response team arrives. The correspondence is extensive: hundreds of letters. They cannot take everything. They must choose what to carry and what to leave behind.

**Twists for Specific Archetypes.** A Celestial Book recognizes the language of the final letter as one that predates the empire entirely. A Cinnabar Heart notices that the lock mechanism has been tampered with recently. Someone else has been here. A Guest Among Forests senses that the nonhuman recipient of the letters is still alive somewhere, and that the letters are keeping a connection open.

**Possible Outcome.** The players retrieve the correspondence and learn that the Alchemist was in contact with an entity that predates the founding of the Court. The entity's final letter contains a warning: "The seal will not hold. It was never meant to hold. It was meant to buy time. You have run out of time." The players must decide who to share this information with and what to do about it.

## The City Breathes

Fifteen atmospheric descriptions of Xiaoyuan at different times and in different moods. Each is ready for the Host to read aloud or paraphrase. Use these to ground a scene, transition between locations, or remind players that the city is a living presence.

### Xiaoyuan at Dawn

"The first light creeps over the city walls like a slow blush. Mist clings to the rooftops. The streets are empty but for sweepers and the night watch heading home. A single bell tolls from the Ministry tower. The smell of steamed buns rises from the first stalls opening in the Outer Market. For this one moment, the city is quiet, and you can almost believe it is at peace. Then the factory whistles start. The day begins."

### Xiaoyuan During a Festival

"The lanterns transform the city into a floating world of light. Every window glows. Every street is lined with paper and silk. The crowd is a single living thing, moving, laughing, eating, dancing. The music is everywhere at once, coming from a dozen directions. You cannot tell where one song ends and the next begins. Children carry tiny lanterns on sticks. Old people sit on doorsteps and watch. For one night, the Court forgets itself. For one night, everyone is just a person."

### Xiaoyuan After an Audit

"The city is holding its breath. The streets are emptier than usual. People walk with their heads down. No one lingers. The teahouses are full of whispered conversations and empty chairs. A document burns in a brazier on every corner. The smoke is grey and smells of old paper. The Commission's seals are everywhere, fresh on doors that were unlocked yesterday. You pass an official you know. They do not meet your eyes. A child runs past, laughing, and a parent snatches them indoors. The Audit has ended. Its shadow has not."

### Xiaoyuan When It Rains

"The rain falls in sheets, turning the streets into rivers of mud and refuse. The gutters overflow. The wooden walkways become slick and treacherous. Everyone huddles under awnings, watching the water pour from the roof edges in curtains. The sound is overwhelming: a roar that fills the world. Steam rises from the wet stone. The smell is clean and raw, the smell of the city's bones. In the rain, Xiaoyuan is honest. You see what it really is beneath the paint and the silk and the lies."

### The Outer Market at Midday

"The heat is thick enough to taste. The air is a stew of frying oil, incense, sweat, and spices. Thousands of voices compete for attention, a constant roar that never dips below a certain threshold. A merchant grabs your sleeve to show you a bolt of silk the color of freshly spilled blood. A child darts past, a stolen apple in their hand. A woman argues with a vegetable seller about the price of leeks. A monk sits motionless in the center of it all, eyes closed, hands folded. The crowd parts around him like water around a stone."

### The Inner Court at Midnight

"The corridors are empty. The Qi lamps have been dimmed to a dull blue glow. Your footsteps echo in the silence. Every door is closed. Every window is dark. But you are not alone. You hear it before you see it: the scratch of a brush on paper, coming from behind a closed door. Someone is working. Someone is always working. The Court never sleeps. It only pretends to, so that the ones who are awake can do what needs to be done without witnesses."

### The Garrison District at Sunset

"The long shadows of the training dummies stretch across the packed earth. The soldiers are finishing their evening drills, their movements synchronized by years of practice. The sound of their feet striking the ground is a single drumbeat, steady and inevitable. A commander shouts a dismissal. The formation breaks into individuals. Some laugh. Some sit down where they stand. Some walk toward the mess hall without speaking. The sun paints the walls orange. The smell of cooking oil and rice drifts from the kitchens. For a moment, the Garrison is just people, tired and hungry and alive."

### The Warrens at Night

"Below the city, another city breathes. The Warrens are a labyrinth of tunnels and chambers carved into the earth over centuries. The air is thick and warm. The only light comes from braziers and the occasional Qi lamp that someone has improvised from a tapped line. People live here. Families. Entire communities of those who fell through the cracks of the Court above. A child stares at you from a doorway. An old woman offers to read your fortune, her eyes milky white, her hands steady. The walls sweat. The floor is uneven. The Warrens remember every foot that has walked them. They remember yours now, too."

### The Spire at Night

"The Spire glows red against the black sky. It is the only thing visible from every corner of the city, a constant reminder of what lies beneath the capital. The red light pulses slowly, steadily, like a heart that does not know it is dying. The pilgrims at the shrine do not sleep. They chant without stopping. Their voices are raw and cracked. Above them, the Spire rises, seamless and silent, into the darkness. You cannot look away. You should not look away. Something inside the Spire is watching you watch it."

### The Imperial Gardens in Autumn

"The leaves have turned. The paths are carpeted with gold and crimson. The ponds reflect the colors of the trees, making the water itself seem to burn. The air is cold and sharp. The Guest Among Forests is here, as she always is, tending to a plant that should not survive this climate. She does not look up as you approach. She is talking to the plant in a language that sounds like wind through leaves. You wait. The plant shivers, or maybe it is just the breeze. She finally turns, and her eyes are the same color as the autumn sky."

### The Foundry District Before Dawn

"The furnaces never go out. The sky above the Foundry District is permanently orange, lit by the glow of molten metal. The workers begin their shift before the sun rises, filing through the gates in a silent river of grey clothes and tired faces. The hammering starts before the gates close behind the last worker. It is a sound that never stops, a heartbeat made of iron. The air tastes of coal and metal. The heat hits you like a wall. This is where the empire makes its tools, its weapons, its chains. This is where the empire makes itself."

### Xiaoyuan in the Snow

"The snow falls silently, transforming the city into something new and innocent. The usual dirt and noise are muffled. The roofs wear white like a blessing. The Spire itself looks less menacing under a layer of frost, its red pulse dimmed by the falling flakes. Children throw snowballs in the streets. Even the guards at the Ministry entrances crack small smiles. The snow does not last. It melts by midday, leaving the city as it was, grey and wet and unchanged. But for those few hours, Xiaoyuan is beautiful. You store the memory away. You will need it on the hard days."

### The Grand Council Chamber in Session

"The doors are closed. You stand in the antechamber with a dozen other petitioners, all of you pretending not to listen to the voices raised inside. The walls are thick, but anger travels through stone. You catch fragments: a school accused of corruption, a budget denied, a motion to censure. The voices rise and fall like a storm. Then silence. The doors open. A scribe emerges, the color drained from his face, and calls the next name. The chamber inside is exactly as you left it. The faces around the circle are exactly as they were. But something has shifted. You can feel it in the air, like the pressure before lightning."

### Xiaoyuan When a Dusk Warning Sounds

"The horns start at the Ministry tower. Low, long notes that cut through every other sound. The streets change instantly. People stop. They look up. They count the notes. Three means a sighting in the outer provinces. Four means a breach. Five means the Dusk is at the walls. The notes keep coming. One. Two. Three. Four. Everyone holds their breath. The fifth note does not come. The fourth hangs in the air and fades. The horns go silent. The city exhales. People resume walking. But they walk faster than before, and no one meets anyone's eyes."

### The River District at Twilight

"The water reflects the dying light in ribbons of orange and grey. The river moves slowly, heavily, carrying the city's waste and its secrets toward the sea. Barges drift past, their pilots silent figures in the stern. On the banks, laundry lines sag between buildings. A woman sings to her child in a language older than the Court. The smell of river water and cooking fires mixes into something that could be called home. The rich build their walls high and their gates strong. But the poor build along the river, where the land is cheap and the water is free. This is where Xiaoyuan lives when it is not performing for the Court."

## Part Six: Campaign Frameworks

### Three Campaign Structures

**The Rise.** The players begin as minor officials in the Outer Court. The campaign follows their ascent through the ranks: first assignment, first faction choice, first Audit, first betrayal, first genuine victory. The focus is on the players' choices shaping their path. The Crimson Dusk is a distant concern, then an approaching one, then the only thing that matters. This structure works best for groups who enjoy gradual power accumulation and watching their decisions compound over time.

**The Conspiracy.** The players begin already entangled in a specific mystery. Perhaps they discover a document that contradicts established doctrine. Perhaps they witness something they should not have. The campaign follows the unraveling of this thread, which leads to larger revelations about the Court, the Dusk, and the Emperor. The focus is on investigation and the question of what to do with dangerous knowledge. This structure works best for groups who enjoy investigation and moral complexity.

**The Collapse.** The Crimson Dusk is already at Phase 2 or 3 when the campaign begins. The empire is actively falling apart. The players are figures who must navigate the catastrophe: protect what can be protected, make impossible choices about what to save, and decide what kind of world should emerge from the ruins. The focus is on survival and legacy. This structure works best for groups who enjoy high stakes and difficult tradeoffs.

### The Rise: Act by Act Breakdown

**Act One: The Outer Court.** The players are newly appointed minor officials in the vast machinery of the Celestial Court. They have a modest shared residence, a small stipend of Celestial Favor, and no real power. Their first assignments are deliberately tedious: filing reports, delivering messages, attending ceremonies. This is not punishment. It is immersion. During this act, the players learn the rhythms of the Court. They encounter minor figures who will become allies or enemies. They choose their first faction affiliations. The Crimson Dusk is a distant concern mentioned in dispatches from the border.

_Situation._ The Ministry of Rites has lost an important document. The players are tasked with finding it. The document is not lost. It was stolen by a clerk who is selling information to a rival school. The players must investigate, make choices about whom to trust, and decide whether to recover the document or use its absence as leverage.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Cinnabar Heart (their section chief), a senior clerk named Lin who knows more than she says, and a Golden Orthodoxy functionary who offers to help for a price.

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Investigate methodically, following the paper trail and interviewing witnesses. 2) Use social connections to bypass the investigation and find the answer through gossip. 3) Plant a fake document and watch who reacts, revealing the thief.

_Two Complications._ 1) The document was stolen by someone the players have already befriended. 2) The missing document is revealed to be one of the forged edicts, and the Cinnabar Heart is watching to see how the players handle the discovery.

**Act Two: The First Alliance.** The players have proven themselves competent. Their Faction Standing is established. Now they must choose deeper commitments. A faction offers them a significant assignment: represent the school's interests in a minor Council dispute. This is a test of loyalty and judgment.

_Situation._ The Verdant Path and the Golden Orthodoxy are in a dispute over funding for a border region that is showing early signs of Dusk contamination. The Verdant Path wants to send resources and investigators. The Golden Orthodoxy wants to quarantine the region and let it be handled by the Commission. The players are assigned to present a third option that benefits their chosen faction.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Guest Among Forests (if the players lean Verdant), a Golden Orthodoxy minister, a representative from the Iron Calculation who cares only about the budget figures.

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Argue for a compromise that gives both sides something they want. 2) Use leverage on the Golden Orthodoxy minister, who has a secret they can exploit. 3) Delay the decision until new information arrives, using the time to strengthen their own position.

_Two Complications._ 1) The Dusk contamination is worse than reported. The border region falls during the debate. 2) A player discovers that their faction deliberately downplayed the Dusk threat to win the argument. They must decide whether to expose the lie.

**Act Three: The First Audit.** The players have accumulated enough influence to attract attention. A Harmonization Audit is called. Everything they have built is at risk. This act is about survival and sacrifice.

_Situation._ The Audit targets the Ministry of Rites, where the players are stationed. The lead investigator is a known enemy of one player's faction. The players must use every resource they have to survive.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Bright Mirror (observing the Audit), the Night Warbler (who may help or hinder), the Iron Calculation (who can provide protection at a price).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Use accumulated Celestial Favor and blackmail material to buy protection. 2) Throw a rival official to the wolves, sacrificing someone else to save themselves. 3) Flee the capital and begin again as fugitives.

_Two Complications._ 1) The Audit was triggered specifically because of something a player did. A past choice comes back directly. 2) A fellow player is named as a target, forcing the party to decide whether to protect each other or save themselves.

**Act Four: The Conspiracy Revealed.** Surviving the Audit puts the players in a new position: they are now among the survivors, the cunning, the dangerous. They begin to see the larger patterns. They uncover evidence of the forged edicts, the Emperor's imprisonment, or the Dusk's true nature.

_Situation._ The players discover a document that proves the Cinnabar Heart has been suppressing information about the forged edicts. He admits the truth and asks for their help. The conspiracy is larger than any one school.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Cinnabar Heart, the Celestial Book (who provides context), the Duke of Eternal Night (who provides historical perspective).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Join the conspiracy and help uncover the full truth. 2) Report the conspiracy to the Bright Mirror and gain his trust. 3) Use the knowledge for personal advantage, blackmailing the conspirators.

_Two Complications._ 1) One of the players is related to a figure who was involved in the original sealing. 2) The Dusk accelerates, making the conspiracy seem suddenly less important than survival.

**Act Five: The Dusk Arrives.** The Crimson Dusk reaches the capital's outskirts. Refugees flood the outer districts. The Court begins to fracture. Political games continue, but the stakes are now existential.

_Situation._ A Dusk event occurs within sight of the capital. The Grand Council is paralyzed. The players must take independent action to protect a district, a faction, or a person they care about.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Iron Wall (organizing the defense), the Iron Bone (mobilizing the workers), the Guest Among Forests (coordinating relief).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Help the Iron Wall with military defense. 2) Help the Iron Bone with civilian evacuation. 3) Use the chaos to infiltrate the Spire of Eternal Vigil.

_Two Complications._ 1) The Dusk event is not natural. It was triggered deliberately to create chaos. 2) A figure the players trusted uses the crisis to seize power, betraying the party.

**Act Six: The Endgame.** The Dusk reaches Phase 4. The empire is in its death throes. The players' accumulated choices determine which endings are available. They must make a final choice: save what can be saved, destroy what must be destroyed, or transcend the conflict entirely.

_Situation._ The Spire is cracking. The seal is failing. The Emperor is waking. The Grand Council has collapsed. The players stand at the center of everything.

_Key NPCs Involved._ All surviving figures, each pursuing their own agenda in the final chaos.

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Attempt to free the Emperor, whatever the cost. 2) Attempt to reinforce the seal, condemning the Emperor but buying time. 3) Attempt to negotiate with the Dusk itself, seeking a third path.

_Two Complications._ 1) A figure who was thought dead reappears with crucial information. 2) The Dusk offers a bargain directly to one player, tempting them with power.

### The Conspiracy: Act by Act Breakdown

**Act One: The Thread.** The players witness or discover something they should not have. A document, a conversation, a death that was not an accident. This thread pulls them into the larger conspiracy.

_Situation._ The players are assigned to inventory a sealed storage room that has not been opened in twenty years. Inside, they find the personal effects of a Disappeared official. Among the effects is a journal that describes a meeting that should not have occurred, between figures who should have been enemies.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Cinnabar Heart (who arranged the assignment), the Night Warbler (who is watching the players now that they have the journal).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Read the journal and follow the leads. 2) Report the discovery to a superior and see what happens. 3) Sell the journal to a faction and let them deal with it.

_Two Complications._ 1) The journal is written in a code that attracts attention when decoded. 2) The Disappeared official is still alive, hidden somewhere, and knows the players have the journal.

**Act Two: The Investigation.** The players follow the thread deeper. They learn that the official was Disappeared not for corruption but for knowing the truth about the Emperor. The conspiracy is real. The players are now part of it.

_Situation._ The players trace the official's last known location to a remote village that has been erased from official maps. They must travel there, avoiding the Commission's agents who are following them.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Guest Among Forests (who provides travel resources), the Shadow (who knows the way), the Commission investigators (who are closing in).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Travel openly, relying on official cover stories. 2) Travel secretly, using back roads and safe houses. 3) Split the party: one group leads the Commission on a false trail while the other reaches the village.

_Two Complications._ 1) The village has been destroyed by the Dusk. 2) The Disappeared official is already dead, but they left a message.

**Act Three: The Choice.** The players have enough information to understand the conspiracy's scope. They know about the forged edicts, the Emperor's imprisonment, or the Dusk's true nature. They must choose what to do with this knowledge.

_Situation._ The Celestial Book confirms the players' findings and reveals the full truth: the founding ritual sacrificed or bound the original guardians and installed the Emperor as their replacement conduit between Heaven and Earth. The founding elders later imprisoned Him, the edicts are forgeries, and completing His seal severed that replacement conduit. The Dusk is the delayed wound created by both acts. The players must decide: reveal the truth, suppress it, or use it.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Celestial Book, the Duke of Eternal Night (who provides the historical account), the Bright Mirror (whose response depends on whether the players can establish authenticity and a reliable chain of custody).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Share the evidence with the Bright Mirror, accepting the rigorous verification and focused investigation that will follow. 2) Share the truth with the Common Flame, knowing they will start a revolution. 3) Keep the secret and work within the system to gradually reform it.

_Two Complications._ 1) A Commission agent has been following the players and knows how much they have learned. 2) The players discover that one of their own was planted by a faction to monitor them.

**Act Four: The Consequence.** The players' choice has consequences. The Court reacts. Factions align and realign. The players are now major figures, whether they wanted to be or not.

_Situation._ Depending on the players' choice, the Court is now in turmoil. If they approached the Bright Mirror, his investigators are authenticating the evidence and pursuing a narrow inquiry, with wider Audits possible only if corroborated proof exposes systemic crimes. If they told the Common Flame, uprisings have begun. If they kept the secret, the conspiracy brings them into its inner circle.

_Key NPCs Involved._ All figures, depending on the choice.

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ Varies based on the situation.

_Two Complications._ 1) A player is captured by the Commission and must be rescued. 2) The Dusk accelerates in response to the chaos.

**Act Five: The Endgame.** The conspiracy reaches its climax. The players must confront the central mystery directly.

_Situation._ The players have a chance to enter the Spire of Eternal Vigil, confront the Emperor, or meet the Dusk directly. The ending depends on everything they have done.

_Key NPCs Involved._ Whoever has survived. The Celestial Book provides final guidance. The Duke provides final warnings.

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Enter the Spire and attempt to free the Emperor. 2) Enter the Spire and attempt to communicate with the Dusk. 3) Refuse to enter the Spire and instead try to reform the Court in the Emperor's absence.

_Two Complications._ 1) The Bright Mirror has been watching and intercepts the players at the Spire's entrance. 2) The Celestial Book dies, taking crucial knowledge with her.

### The Collapse: Act by Act Breakdown

**Act One: The First Fall.** The campaign begins with a crisis. A major city falls to the Dusk. The players are among those tasked with responding. They are thrown into chaos with no time to prepare.

_Situation._ The players are in a border city when the Dusk advances faster than predicted. They must evacuate, survive, and make impossible choices about who to save.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Iron Wall (commanding the evacuation), the Iron Bone (organizing civilian resistance), local officials (who are overwhelmed or corrupt).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Help the Iron Wall with the military evacuation. 2) Help the Iron Bone with civilian escape routes. 3) Use the chaos to acquire resources and information from abandoned buildings.

_Two Complications._ 1) A player's contact or ally is trapped in the Dusk zone. 2) The retreat is ordered too late, and the players are cut off.

**Act Two: The Capital in Crisis.** The players reach the capital, which is now flooded with refugees and facing shortages. The Court is fracturing. Old rules no longer apply.

_Situation._ The capital is under martial law. Resources are rationed. The Grand Council meets constantly but accomplishes nothing. The players must find their place in this new, harsher world.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Iron Calculation (managing resources), the Golden Orthodoxy (enforcing order), the Guest Among Forests (running refugee camps).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Join the relief effort, building goodwill among the people. 2) Work the black market, building resources for the coming collapse. 3) Infiltrate the Grand Council's emergency sessions, gathering intelligence.

_Two Complications._ 1) A riot breaks out in a refugee camp, and the players are caught in it. 2) A Grand Council member is assassinated, and the players are suspects.

**Act Three: The Fracture.** The Court splits. A faction attempts a coup, or a region declares independence, or the Iron Wall executes Operation Empty Throne. The players must choose a side.

_Situation._ The Golden Orthodoxy attempts to seize absolute control, citing the emergency. The other factions resist. The capital becomes a battlefield, not of armies but of assassins, propaganda, and economic warfare.

_Key NPCs Involved._ All figures, each forced to choose a side or betray their principles.

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Support the Golden Orthodoxy's takeover, hoping for stability. 2) Join the resistance, fighting for a reformed Court. 3) Attempt to remain neutral, protecting civilians from both sides.

_Two Complications._ 1) The Duke of Eternal Night reveals he has been manipulating both sides. 2) The Dusk reaches the capital's outer walls during the conflict.

**Act Four: The Siege.** The Dusk surrounds the capital. There is no escape. The players must defend what they can and decide what is worth saving.

_Situation._ The capital is under siege by the Dusk. The walls hold, but the situation inside is desperate. Food is running out. The Dusk's influence seeps in, affecting dreams, causing madness, revealing truths.

_Key NPCs Involved._ The Iron Wall (leading the defense), the Bright Mirror (hunting traitors), the Cinnabar Heart (burning documents).

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Fight on the walls, holding back the Dusk physically. 2) Search for a solution rooted in Qi or ritual to push back the Dusk. 3) Attempt to negotiate with the Dusk or the Emperor, seeking an end to the siege.

_Two Complications._ 1) A player begins to show signs of Dusk touch. 2) The food stores are discovered to have been poisoned, or stolen, or never existed.

**Act Five: The End.** The walls fall, or they hold. The Emperor is freed, or He is not. The Dusk consumes the capital, or it recedes. The players face the final consequences of their choices.

_Situation._ The final confrontation. The Spire is the last stronghold, or the Dusk recedes when a specific condition is met, or the Emperor emerges and makes a final judgment.

_Key NPCs Involved._ Whoever remains. The survivors gather at the Spire, in the Grand Council Chamber, or in the ruins of the Outer Market.

_Three Possible Player Approaches._ 1) Make a final stand against the Dusk, buying time for others to escape. 2) Enter the Spire and confront whatever is inside. 3) Lead a mass evacuation through a hidden passage, saving as many as possible.

_Two Complications._ 1) A figure the players trusted betrays them at the final moment. 2) The players discover that the Dusk arose from both the founding ritual and the later sealing, and that healing the wound may require one of them to take the place of the severed conduit.

### Pacing the Crimson Dusk

The Dusk advances at the Host's discretion. Use it to:

- **Create urgency** when the players have been comfortable too long. A Dusk event in a previously safe province reminds everyone what is at stake.
- **Force difficult choices.** When resources are scarce because a province has fallen, who gets them?
- **Reveal information.** Dusk Touched individuals sometimes speak truths. A refugee from a fallen province might know something crucial.
- **Trigger the endgame.** When the Dusk reaches Phase 3, the tone shifts. Maneuvering matters less. Choosing matters more.

### The Endgame

When the Dusk reaches Phase 4, the campaign reaches its climax. The players' accumulated choices determine which endings are possible. The Host should present the available endings based on what the players have done, not as a menu but as consequences that flow naturally from their actions:

- Have they uncovered the truth about the Emperor? Freeing Him becomes possible.
- Have they built strong alliances across factional lines? A coalition ending is possible.
- Have they burned every bridge? A solitary ending emerges.
- Have they learned the Dusk's true nature? Transcendence becomes possible.

The ending is not a reward. It is a consequence. The players should feel that the ending they get is the ending they earned.

## Part Eight: Adjudication

### Handling Player Creativity

Players will try things you did not anticipate. This is the point. When a player proposes an action not covered by the rules:

1. **Determine if a roll is needed.** Is the outcome uncertain? Is failure interesting? If no to either, narrate the result without rolling.
2. **Identify the relevant Facet and Skill.** What capability is the player drawing on? If nothing fits perfectly, choose the closest match.
3. **Set a Target Number.** Use the TN table as a guideline, not a straitjacket.
4. **Announce the TN and the stakes.** "If you fail, the guard will raise the alarm. Roll Swiftness plus Stealth against TN 16."
5. **Resolve and narrate.** Describe the outcome. Make it vivid.

### Adjudicating Qi Techniques

Players may attempt to use Qi in creative ways beyond their known Techniques. When this happens:

1. The player describes what they want to achieve.
2. The Host determines the Qi cost (use existing Techniques as benchmarks).
3. The Host calls for a Qi Theory check against an appropriate TN.
4. On success, the effect occurs. On failure, Qi Disharmony (see Chapter Seven).

### Adjudicating Faction Consequences

When players take actions that affect the factional balance:

1. Ask: which schools benefit from this? Which schools are harmed?
2. Adjust Faction Standing for the affected schools. Small actions shift Faction Standing by 1 to 3 points. Major actions shift it by 5 to 10.
3. Consider: does any school have reason to retaliate? The retaliation may not come immediately. The Court has a long memory.
4. Tell the players what they observe. They may notice a school suddenly becoming warmer or colder. They may not know why.

### When the Rules Get in the Way

A rule that produces an unsatisfying result at the table is a rule that should be ignored in that moment. Make a note. After the session, decide whether the rule needs modification or whether the specific situation was an exception. Your table's enjoyment is the only rule that cannot be overridden.

### Adjudication Scenarios

Each scenario below presents a situation, what the rules say, how the Host adjudicated it, and the outcome. Use these as models for your own rulings.

**Scenario One: Creative Qi Use.** The player wants to use their Qi to amplify their voice across the entire Outer Market, creating a distraction while their ally steals a document from a nearby stall.

_What the Rules Say._ Qi Techniques are defined by the player's known abilities. Amplifying voice is not a listed Technique. The player argues that their Qi Theory skill should allow an improvised application.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host agreed that Qi can be shaped in creative ways. The player described how they would channel Qi to their throat and modulate their breath. The Host set a Qi Theory TN of 16 and a Qi cost of 3 points, equivalent to a minor Technique. On success, the voice amplification would last one minute. On failure, the player's voice would vanish completely for an hour.

_Outcome._ The player rolled a 14. They failed the check. Their voice disappeared. They could not call out to their ally or warn them of danger. The ally, expecting a distraction that never came, had to improvise. The theft succeeded but was sloppy, leaving evidence that later led to a Commission investigation. The player's creative attempt created an interesting complication that drove the next session.

**Scenario Two: Social PvP.** One player wants to convince an NPC that another player is a Dusk spy. The targeted player has done nothing to warrant this accusation. The accusing player has a personal grudge against the other player's character.

_What the Rules Say._ Social skills against other players are not covered by the standard rules. The system assumes players cooperate.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host paused the game and talked to both players privately. They confirmed that both were comfortable with conflict between their characters. The Host then set a contested roll: the accusing player's Presence plus Persuasion against the targeted player's Presence plus Deception. The Host announced the stakes: if the accusation succeeded, the target would lose 10 Celestial Favor and be flagged for investigation. If it failed, the accuser would lose standing with the NPC.

_Outcome._ The accusing player rolled well. The targeted player rolled poorly. The NPC believed the accusation. The targeted player was investigated for two sessions, losing Celestial Favor and access. The conflict created excellent roleplay. The targeted player eventually cleared their name and sought revenge. The table agreed afterward that the conflict had made the game better.

**Scenario Three: Player Betrayal.** A player has been secretly feeding information to the Golden Orthodoxy. The other players discover this during a session. The betraying player has been planning this for weeks.

_What the Rules Say._ Player betrayal is not covered by rules. It is a table dynamic issue.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host called a break and spoke to the betraying player privately. They confirmed the player wanted this to be a story moment, not a character exit. The Host then worked with the player to orchestrate a dramatic reveal. The betraying player would confess under pressure, explain their reasons, and offer a chance for redemption. The Host also prepared a backup plan: if the table reacted with hostility, the betraying character would become an NPC antagonist.

_Outcome._ The reveal happened during a tense Council scene. The other players were shocked but listened to the betrayer's reasons: they were trying to protect their family from the Golden Orthodoxy's threats. The table decided to give the character a chance to earn back trust. The betraying player played the redemption arc beautifully. The moment became one of the campaign's most memorable scenes.

**Scenario Four: Last Stand.** A player's character is cornered by Commission investigators in a narrow alley. There are six investigators. The player has no allies nearby and no escape route. The player says: "I want to make a last stand. I want to go down fighting, but I want to take as many of them with me as possible."

_What the Rules Say._ Standard combat rules would resolve this as a normal fight. The character would likely die without achieving much.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host declared this a "Last Stand" scene, using a special rule created on the spot. The player would make a series of three rolls: Attack, Defense, and Resolve. Each success would kill one investigator and buy time for the player's allies elsewhere. Each failure would reduce the number of investigators the player could hold off. The player would die at the end of the scene regardless of the rolls, but their success determined how much their sacrifice achieved.

_Outcome._ The player rolled two successes and one failure. They killed three investigators and wounded a fourth before falling. The time they bought allowed their allies to escape a trap. The player's new character entered the next session, having been inspired by the legendary last stand of their predecessor.

**Scenario Five: Negotiation with a Dusk Entity.** The players encounter a Dusk Touched individual who seems to be aware and communicative. It speaks in fragments. It offers to help them in exchange for something. The players want to negotiate.

_What the Rules Say._ The Dusk is described as something that cannot be reasoned with. The rules explicitly state the Dusk does not negotiate.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host ruled that this Dusk entity is an anomaly, a fragment that retained enough consciousness to communicate. The negotiation was possible but dangerous. The Host set a Presence plus Insight roll against TN 20, the highest difficulty in the game. On success, the players would learn a crucial piece of information about the Dusk's origin. On failure, the entity would take control of one player character for a scene.

_Outcome._ The lead negotiator rolled a natural 20. The entity spoke clearly for the first time. It told them: "I was a scholar. I studied the Spire. I found a crack. I looked inside. What looked back is what I became. Do not look inside." The entity then crumbled to dust. The information was invaluable for the players' investigation.

**Scenario Six: The Impossible Heist.** The players want to steal a document from the Bright Mirror's personal office. The office is in the Commission headquarters, guarded by elite Justicars, protected by Qi wards. The players have no inside contact and no plan beyond "we are very sneaky."

_What the Rules Say._ The difficulty would normally make this effectively impossible. The TN would be absurdly high.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host did not say no. They asked the players to come up with a plan. If the players could describe a method that addressed the wards, the guards, and the document's location, the Host would set reasonable TNs. The Host also hinted that there might be alternative approaches: blackmail a Commission official, create a diversion requiring a school's resources, or wait until the Bright Mirror was called away.

_Outcome._ The players spent an entire session planning. They recruited a disgruntled Commission clerk, bribed a guard, triggered a false Dusk alarm that drew the Bright Mirror away, and used a talisman that dampens Qi to bypass the wards. The heist succeeded. The document revealed that the Bright Mirror had been investigating the same conspiracy the players were investigating. He was not their enemy. He was their unknowing ally.

**Scenario Seven: The Impossible Persuasion.** A player wants to convince the Duke of Eternal Night to reveal what he knows about the Emperor's imprisonment. The Duke has kept this secret for roughly a century and a half. He has refused every request from every major figure.

_What the Rules Say._ Persuasion rules exist but are designed for ordinary interactions. This is not ordinary.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host told the player that a single Persuasion roll would not work. This would require a longer process. The player needed to earn the Duke's trust through multiple interactions: share a secret of their own, do him a genuine service without asking for payment, demonstrate that they understand the weight of what they are asking. Only after these conditions were met would the Host allow a single final Persuasion roll at TN 18.

_Outcome._ The player spent three sessions building the relationship. They shared a personal secret about their past. They retrieved a lost heirloom for the Duke. They demonstrated that they understood the consequences of the knowledge. On the third session, the Duke finally told them the truth: the Emperor was not imprisoned by enemies but by allies who believed they were saving the empire. The Duke voted against them, failed to stop the sealing, and later failed to help the rescue attempt. He has regretted both failures ever since.

**Scenario Eight: The Chaotic Party Split.** Half the players are in a Council session trying to prevent a war vote. The other half are breaking into the Commission archives. Both halves need simultaneous resolution.

_What the Rules Say._ Standard turn order cannot handle simultaneous scenes in different locations.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host split the table physically, sending half the players to a separate room (or separate end of the table) with a notepad. The Host moved between the two groups at intervals of ten minutes, resolving three to four rounds of action at a time. The groups could not communicate with each other. The players in the Council session had to stall. The players in the archives had to find what they needed and escape.

_Outcome._ The Council group succeeded in delaying the vote by introducing a procedural motion. The archive group found the evidence they needed but triggered an alarm in the process. The alarm reached the Council chamber just as the vote was about to begin. The resulting chaos gave the players exactly the opening they needed. The two groups reunited triumphantly, each having succeeded because of the other's actions.

**Scenario Nine: The Unfair Death.** A player character dies in a random encounter that had nothing to do with the main plot. A guard got a lucky critical hit. The player is unhappy. The death feels meaningless.

_What the Rules Say._ Dice results are final. The character is dead.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host acknowledged the player's frustration. They offered an option: the character could survive, but with a permanent consequence. The guard's blade severed something important, a tendon, a nerve, a Qi channel. The character would lose one point of a relevant Attribute permanently. The player would get to keep the character, but the near death would leave a mark. The player accepted.

_Outcome._ The character survived with a limp and a reduced Swiftness score. The limp became a defining trait. The player roleplayed the character's recovery beautifully, turning a random death into a story about survival and adaptation. The table agreed that the compromise honored the dice while preserving the player's investment.

**Scenario Ten: The Unexpected Alliance.** A player proposes an alliance between the Common Flame and the Crimson Lineage, two schools that have been enemies for generations. The player has contacts in both schools. They want to bring them together against the Dusk.

_What the Rules Say._ No rules cover forming alliances across factions. The faction system assumes schools operate independently.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host asked the player to describe how they would approach each school. For the Common Flame, the player emphasized practical cooperation: the Dusk does not care about lineage or ideology. For the Crimson Lineage, the player appealed to legacy: if the empire falls, the lineage falls with it. The Host set a Presence plus Persuasion roll for each school, TN 16. Both rolls were needed.

_Outcome._ The player succeeded on both rolls. The alliance formed, tentatively. The Common Flame provided workers and supplies. The Crimson Lineage provided ancient knowledge and Qi resources. The alliance held for three sessions before internal tensions broke it apart. But during those three sessions, the players accomplished more than any single school could have. The alliance became a model for what the empire could become if it set aside its divisions.

**Scenario Eleven: Alternative Facet Social Approach.** The player wants to convince a suspicious merchant to share information about a smuggling operation. Instead of using Presence (Persuasion or Deception) as the rules default, the player argues their character would use Swiftness to demonstrate a physical skill, impressing the merchant through action rather than words.

_What the Rules Say._ Social encounters default to skills governed by Presence. The player is proposing a different Facet entirely, which is not covered by the standard social interaction rules.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host agreed that demonstration of skill can be a legitimate social approach, just an unconventional one. The player described their character picking the merchant's complex lockbox in three seconds flat, opening it, closing it, and handing it back without taking anything. The Host set the action as Swiftness plus Larceny against TN 16. The stakes were different from a standard Persuasion roll: success would not make the merchant friendly, but it would make them impressed and cautious. They would share information because they wanted the skilled operative on their side rather than against them. Failure would not merely fail to impress; it would make the merchant afraid and defensive, calling for guards instead of talking.

_Outcome._ The player rolled a 17. The lock clicked open in their hands. The merchant's eyes widened. "I could use someone with your talents," they said, and slid a document across the table. "This is the shipping schedule you want. I ask only that you remember who helped you." The player gained the information without making a social promise or creating a debt, but the merchant now knows what the player is capable of and will spread that reputation through the underground. The unconventional approach succeeded, but it changed how the NPC sees the player.

**Scenario Twelve: Combat Ability Outside Combat.** The player, an Iron Bone, wants to use their Wolf Rises ability during an interrogation of a captured Commission agent. The ability is mechanically designed for combat transformation, but the player argues that the threat of the wolf is a social tool, not just a weapon.

_What the Rules Say._ Wolf Rises is a combat Technique that transforms the character and has defined mechanical effects for combat only. It has no explicit social application in the rules.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host ruled that the wolf inside the Iron Bone is not just a combat transformation; it is a presence that others can sense even when the character is in human form. The player did not need to fully transform. They let the wolf show in their eyes, their voice, the subtle shift of their posture. The Host set this as a contested roll: the player's Presence plus Intimidation (with Advantage because of the wolf's supernatural menace) against the agent's Resolve plus Insight. The stakes were layered: immediate success would end the interrogation with the agent talking freely, but the agent would report the encounter to the Commission, flagging the player as a potential threat for future Audits.

_Outcome._ The player rolled well. The captured Commission agent felt something ancient and hungry looking out through the Iron Bone's eyes. The agent talked. The player got the information they needed. But the agent's report to the Commission included the phrase "subject displayed unnatural aggression." The Commission placed a note in the player's permanent file. Months later, when an Audit came, that note increased the player's Risk by one level. The creative solution worked in the moment but created a consequence that resonated into future sessions, a fair trade for using a combat ability as a social weapon.

**Scenario Thirteen: Player Backstory Contradicts Adventure Assumptions.** The adventure assumes the Shadow is an elusive NPC that the players must track down and negotiate with for information about a conspiracy. One player has chosen the Shadow archetype, and their backstory establishes them as the only living figure operating under that name. The adventure's plot cannot proceed as written because the figure the players are supposed to find is standing right next to them.

_What the Rules Say._ No rules cover a situation where a player's archetype duplicates a major NPC referenced in the adventure structure. The adventure was written assuming the Shadow NPC would be an external figure to discover and interact with.

_How the Host Adjudicated._ The Host paused and reworked the plot rather than telling the player their chosen archetype broke the adventure. The Shadow NPC became a different entity: the previous holder of the Shadow title who had emerged from retirement, now using the player's identity for their own purposes. The player's character now had a personal stake in the confrontation: this imposter was not just an obstacle, but someone actively damaging the reputation the player had built. The Host also seeded a deeper mystery: perhaps the adventure's assumed NPC was always meant to be this player's character, and the written framework was merely predicting a role the player would naturally fill.

_Outcome._ The player became the center of the arc rather than a participant in someone else's. The pretender Shadow was revealed to be a former apprentice who had stolen the player's methods and identity after the player escaped the Warrens for the Court. The confrontation was personal and emotional, not a simple negotiation with an anonymous informant. The other players rallied around their ally, and the adventure's information was obtained through a dramatic reckoning rather than a transactional exchange. The Host's flexibility turned a potential plot hole into the campaign arc with the strongest focus on a single character.

## Part Nine: A Guide to Your First Session

### The Night Before

Your first session sets the tone for the entire campaign. Preparation is simple but specific. Read the Court Event Generator and the NPC Quick Generator in Part Seven. You will improvise most of the first session. That is normal. Prepare three things and nothing more. First, prepare the players' starting situation: where they are, what they are doing, and who they report to. Second, prepare one NPC they will definitely meet: their supervisor, a contact, or a rival. Third, prepare one situation they can engage with: a document to find, a person to meet, a problem to solve. Everything else you will generate at the table.

Print or prepare the following for yourself: the Host Quick Reference from the Appendix, the Rumor Table, and the Court Event Generator. Have a sheet of paper for the relationship map. Have index cards for NPC names and details. Do not prepare more than this. Overpreparation leads to a rigid session. Your players will surprise you. You need room to respond.

### Setting Up the Table

Arrive ten minutes early. Arrange your space so you can see all players and all players can see each other. You at the head or center is traditional. Have your reference sheets where you can glance at them without breaking eye contact. Have a bowl of snacks within reach but not at the center of the table. The center of the table is for the game.

Place three items in the center of the table: a candle (lit, if the space allows, or an LED candle for safety), a small bowl of coins or stones representing Celestial Favor (the players will add to it and spend from it as the game progresses), and a single piece of paper with the words "The Crimson Dusk Advances" written on it. This last item is a visual reminder. When the Dusk advances, you will mark the paper. The players will see it. They will feel the pressure.

### Starting the Session: The First Five Minutes

Begin with these exact words, or something close to them. Read them aloud. Let the silence settle afterward.

"You are in the Celestial Court. The empire has gone about one hundred and sixty four years without hearing its Emperor speak in public. The Crimson Dusk spreads slowly across the border provinces. The Grand Council governs in the Emperor's absence. The Six Schools compete for influence. And you are here, in the heart of it all, as minor officials in a vast machine that does not know your name. Yet."

Pause. Let the players feel the weight.

"Your first assignment has arrived. A scroll bearing the seal of your bureau is placed on your desk. It contains orders, a location, and a deadline. You do not know what you will find when you arrive. That is the nature of the Court. You are told what to do. You discover what it means after you arrive."

Ask each player: "What is your character's name and title?" Write them down. Ask: "What does your character carry with them that no one else knows about?" This is the first seed of personal story.

Then describe the first scene. Keep it simple. "The corridor is cold. The floor is worn marble. You pass other officials who do not meet your eyes. The office you are looking for is at the end of the hall. The door is slightly ajar." Then ask: "What do you do?"

### Introducing the World

Do not explain the entire setting in the first session. The players should learn the Court the same way their characters did: by experiencing it. When an official uses a title they do not understand, they ask. When a document mentions a school they have not heard of, they investigate. When a rumor references an event from the Court's history, they discover it through play.

In the first session, establish these three things only. One, the Court is vast and impersonal. There are thousands of officials, endless corridors, and a bureaucracy that does not care about individuals. Two, information is power. Everyone is watching everyone. A careless word can destroy a career. Three, the Crimson Dusk exists. It is distant, it is dangerous, and it is the reason the empire is afraid.

Do not mention the forged edicts, the Emperor's imprisonment, or the Dusk's true nature. Those are discoveries for later sessions. Let the players develop curiosity naturally.

### Managing Pacing

The first session should have a clear rhythm. Begin with a scene that establishes the setting: the players receive their assignment. Follow with a scene that introduces challenge: something goes wrong, a complication arises, or an NPC tests them. End with a scene that creates a hook: a mystery, a threat, or an opportunity that makes the players want to return for the next session.

Keep each scene to fifteen or twenty minutes. If a scene is dragging, introduce a complication. A messenger arrives with urgent news. A guard enters with a question. An alarm sounds in the distance. You can also simply narrate past the slow part. "You spend the next hour searching the archives. You find nothing useful. Then, as you are about to leave, you notice something."

Trust that you can end a scene. Say "the scene ends there" if the players are stuck. Move to the next scene. The momentum is more important than completeness.

### Ending the Session

End the session on a question. The traditional approach is: "You have the document in your hands. Through the window, you see the sun setting. You hear footsteps in the corridor. Someone is coming. What do you do?" Then pause. Say: "And that is where we will pick up next time."

This gives the players something to think about between sessions. They will arrive next time already engaged, already planning, already eager to play.

Before you close, ask each player two questions. "What was your favorite moment tonight?" and "What is your character's goal for next session?" Write down the answers. Use them to prepare.

### Between Sessions

Spend fifteen minutes after each session updating the relationship map. Add any new connections the players formed. Remove any that have become irrelevant. Note which figures know about the players and what they think.

Spend another fifteen minutes preparing one situation for the next session. Do not plan a plot. Plan a situation: a problem the players might encounter, with a few possible solutions written down. The players will find a different solution. That is fine. The situation is your anchor. Their solution is the story.

Review the player answers from the end of the previous session. If a player said their character wants to investigate a specific rumor, prepare that rumor. If a player said their favorite moment was interacting with a certain NPC, bring that NPC back. The players are telling you what they enjoy. Listen.

Do not prepare more than thirty minutes between sessions. The game should not feel like homework. Your enthusiasm matters more than your preparation. If you are excited to run the next session, the players will be excited to play it.

## Part Ten: When Things Go Wrong

Even the best Hosts face difficult moments. This section covers the most common problems and how to handle them with grace.

### When Players Are Stuck

The players have stopped discussing options. They are looking at you expectantly. They do not know what to do next. This is not failure. It is a signal that you need to provide more information.

Give them something concrete. A messenger arrives with a new development. A NPC they trust appears and offers guidance. An event occurs that forces a decision. You can also simply tell them: "Your character would know that the archives are located in the East Wing. You remember passing them earlier." The characters know things the players do not. Remind them of what their characters would know.

If the players are stuck because they missed a clue, do not let the story stall. Move the clue to a new location. The document they needed to find is no longer in the locked drawer. It is now in the hands of a courier who is looking for them. The players do not need to know you moved it. They only need to know the story continues.

### When Combat Is Too Hard or Too Easy

Combat in the Violet Court is not the primary focus, but it happens. If a fight is too easy, end it quickly. Narrate the final blows. "You cut through the last guard. The remaining three throw down their weapons." Do not drag out a decided encounter.

If a fight is too hard, offer the players an out. A door they did not notice. A negotiation opportunity. A character who offers to surrender in exchange for information. You can also lower enemy stats during combat. The players do not have your notes. They will not know that the guard captain originally had more health. They will only know that the fight was tense but winnable.

### When a Player Is Dominating the Table

One player speaks more than the others. They make decisions for the group. They interrupt. The quieter players are disengaging. This is a common problem and requires gentle correction.

Use direct but respectful techniques. Address quieter players by name. "Lena, what does your character think about this plan?" When the dominant player interrupts, pause and say: "Let's hear from others first." Create scenes that require the quieter player's specific skills. If the dominant player is a negotiator, design a scene that requires stealth or lore knowledge, forcing another player to take the lead.

If the problem persists, speak to the dominant player privately. Say: "I appreciate your enthusiasm. I need help making sure everyone gets equal time. Can you help me with that?" Most players do not realize they are dominating. A gentle nudge is enough.

### When the Story Goes Completely Off the Rails

The players have abandoned your prepared situation entirely. They have decided to sail to a distant province you have not detailed. They are starting a restaurant instead of investigating the conspiracy. They have adopted a stray dog and are more interested in it than the Crimson Dusk.

First, breathe. This is normal. This is good. Player agency is the point.

Second, assess whether you can adapt. Can the situation you prepared come to them? The document they needed to find follows them. The NPC they were supposed to meet seeks them out. The conspiracy does not stop because the players are ignoring it. It continues in the background. Let them hear rumors of events they missed.

Third, ask yourself whether the new direction is interesting. If the players are genuinely engaged with a new goal, follow them. Build situations around their new interest. The Court's bureaucracy is everywhere. Even a restaurant in a distant province would be subject to regulations, inspections, and factional pressure. The game is wherever the players are.

### When a Character Death Feels Unfair

Sometimes the dice produce a death that feels arbitrary. A lucky critical. A failed save against a minor threat. The player is frustrated. The death does not serve the story.

You have options. Offer a permanent consequence instead of death, as described in the adjudication scenarios. Allow the character to survive but with a debt, a scar, or a loss of standing. Let the character make a deal with an entity they were not supposed to negotiate with. The Duke of Eternal Night might offer resurrection, at a price.

If the player genuinely wants the character to die despite the unfairness, make the death meaningful. Give them a final scene. Let them say goodbye. Let them choose how they fall. A character who dies saving an ally, or delivering a crucial message, or cursing their killer with their last breath, does not die unfairly. They die memorably.

Then help the player create a new character who is connected to the party. The new character arrives with a purpose, a secret, and a reason to trust the surviving players. The dead character is mourned within the game and remembered away from the table. Their death becomes part of the story.

### How to Handle Sensitive Content

The Violet Court deals with difficult themes: oppression, corruption, betrayal, death. These are part of the game's design. They can also be genuinely upsetting to players.

Before the campaign begins, use the Consent Checklist from the core book. Identify what topics individual players want to avoid entirely, what topics they are comfortable with handled carefully, and what topics are fully acceptable. Write these down. Keep them accessible during sessions.

During play, use the "pause and check" technique. If a scene is approaching a sensitive topic, pause and ask: "Is everyone comfortable continuing this scene?" Give players permission to say no. The response "I think we should fade to black here" should always be honored without question.

If a player becomes visibly uncomfortable during a scene, pause the game. Check in with them privately. Offer to retcon, skip, or alter the scene. The player's wellbeing is more important than the story. Always.

Use the "X Card" system: place a card in the center of the table. Any player can tap it at any time to stop a scene that is bothering them. No explanation is needed. No questions are asked. The scene ends. This is not a criticism of the Host. It is a tool for table safety.

Remember that the goal is for everyone to have fun. The game's dark themes serve the story. They do not serve anything else. If a theme is not serving the table's enjoyment, set it aside. There are infinite stories you could tell in the Violet Court. Find the ones that work for your group.

## Part Eleven: Adapting Adventures for Your Table

No adventure survives contact with a specific group of players unchanged. This section provides tools for adapting any Violet Court scenario to your table's unique composition. The adventure as written is a starting point. Your players will depart from it within minutes. The art of hosting is knowing when to follow and when to gently steer.

### When Archetypes Have No Obvious Role

Some adventures assume specific archetypes will handle certain challenges. When your table lacks those archetypes, adapt the challenge rather than force a player into an unsuitable role:

**No infiltrator available.** If the adventure expects a Shadow or Night Warbler to sneak into a location but no player has those skills, change the nature of the challenge. The infiltration becomes a social engineering problem: who can be bribed, blackmailed, or persuaded to grant access? Create an NPC who needs the players' help and whose access can be borrowed. The obstacle changes from "how do we get in unseen" to "who do we know who can get us in." The same dramatic tension remains, but the approach shifts to strengths the table actually possesses.

**No investigator available.** If the adventure expects a Celestial Book or Bright Mirror to unravel a mystery but your table lacks dedicated investigators, make the clues more accessible through other means. A witness offers testimony unprompted because they recognize a player's reputation. A document arrives with an anonymous annotation explaining its significance. The mystery does not need to be easier; it needs to be findable by the skills at the table. Let combat characters intimidate witnesses for information. Let social characters charm answers out of reluctant sources. Let the Iron Calculation solve the problem through raw data analysis.

**No martial character available.** If the adventure includes combat encounters but your table has no Iron Wall or Iron Bone, transform those encounters. The armed guards become a puzzle: how do we get past them without fighting? The assassin becomes a target for social manipulation: who hired them, and can we flip them? The physical confrontation becomes an environmental hazard: the collapsing bridge must be crossed, and no one is trained to hold it. Each of these maintains the dramatic stakes while respecting the party's capabilities.

**No social specialist available.** If the adventure requires delicate negotiations and no player has invested in social skills, change the negotiation's terms. The faction representative cares more about practical demonstrations of competence than eloquent speech. A display of lore knowledge, a solved problem, or a delivered package speaks louder than words. The encounter becomes a transaction rather than a persuasion. The NPC responds to results, not rhetoric.

**The principle is simple.** Translate the challenge into a Facet someone at the table excels at. The obstacle and its stakes remain; the method changes. Players should feel that their choices of archetype matter, and that the world adapts to who they are rather than demanding they be someone else.

### Adding Combat Beats for Martial Characters During Intrigue Sessions

Sessions dominated by intrigue can leave players who favor combat feeling sidelined. These players chose their archetype because they enjoy moments of physical danger and decisive action. Even in a session dominated by politics and conversation, you can create moments that showcase their abilities:

**The duel of honor.** A minor official insults the party or a faction they represent. The only acceptable response in Court etiquette is a formal challenge. The martial character steps forward. The duel is bound by rules: first blood, submission, or a specific number of exchanges. This is not a fight to the death. It is a demonstration of skill that carries social weight. The outcome affects how the faction views the entire party.

**The sudden ambush.** In the middle of a negotiation or investigation, attackers strike. They may be rivals who want to eliminate competition, or a faction sending a message. The martial character's combat readiness saves the party. The fight should be brief but dramatic, three or four rounds at most, showcasing the character's abilities before the enemy retreats or is defeated. The other characters contribute through tactics, support, or protecting the objective.

**The physical obstacle.** A door that must be forced open. A collapse that must be held while others escape. A pursuit through the city rooftops. These are not combat encounters, but they require the physical capabilities that martial characters possess. Describe them with the same intensity as a fight. The Iron Wall holding a falling ceiling while others escape is as dramatic as any battle.

**The display of force.** Sometimes the most effective intimidation is not a threat but a demonstration. The martial character breaks a training dummy in half with a single blow, or casually lifts a cart that two men could not move, or throws a dagger that pins a document to the wall fifteen paces away. The room understands the message without a word being spoken.

**The bodyguard moment.** An NPC the party needs to protect is targeted. The martial character positions themselves between the danger and the target. This is a defensive scene that showcases the character's protective instincts and combat readiness. Let them feel the satisfaction of being the wall that cannot be broken.

### Handling the Shadow Archetype When the Adventure References the Shadow NPC

The Shadow NPC occupies a unique position in the Violet Court: a major figure who is also a playable archetype. When a player chooses the Shadow, every adventure that references the Shadow NPC requires adjustment. The following approaches give you options depending on your table:

**The player IS the Shadow.** The simplest solution: the player's character is the figure referred to in the adventure. The adventure's notes about meeting the Shadow become notes about the player's own past involvement. The faction seeking the Shadow becomes a faction seeking the player. This works best when the player enjoys being the center of attention and the rest of the table is comfortable with that role. It gives the player the strongest connection to the world.

**The Shadow is a legacy title.** The player holds the title and role, but a previous holder of the title still exists and may emerge from retirement. Adventure references to "the Shadow" refer to the previous holder, not the player. The player's actions build their own reputation separate from the legacy. This creates interesting dynamics: does the player live up to the title, exceed it, or reject it? Does the previous holder approve of the player's choices?

**There is a pretender.** Someone else is using the Shadow's name and reputation to accomplish their own goals. The player must decide whether to expose the pretender, absorb their operation, or let them continue and take credit for their successes. This creates a unique antagonist who is a dark mirror of the player character, someone who wants what the player has without earning it.

**The Shadow is a construct of reputation.** The Shadow is not a single person but a collection of deeds attributed to a mythical figure. Multiple operatives over the years have committed acts credited to "the Shadow." The player is the latest in this tradition. Adventures referencing the Shadow reference the legend, not any specific individual. The player must decide whether to maintain the legend, reveal the truth, or reshape what the Shadow means.

When adapting, communicate with the Shadow player privately about which approach they prefer. Their comfort with being the narrative focus determines how much adjustment the adventure needs.

### Balancing Spotlight Time Across Six Very Different Characters

The Violet Court supports a wide range of character types. When your table has multiple players with different archetypes, ensuring everyone gets meaningful spotlight time requires deliberate design:

**Rotate the primary challenge.** Each session or story arc, design the central challenge around a different character's strengths. One session features a negotiation the Bright Mirror must lead. The next features an infiltration the Night Warbler must execute. The next features a puzzle the Celestial Book must solve. Players learn to support each other and step back when someone else's skills are needed. The rotation does not need to be rigid, but it should be visible enough that each player feels their moment is coming.

**Build scenes with layered challenges.** A single scene can serve multiple characters at once. A negotiation with a Crimson Lineage representative tests the Duke of Eternal Night's ancient authority while also being the moment the Iron Bone must control their temper and the Cinnabar Heart must spot the forgery in the documents being presented. Every character has something to do in the same scene, and no one feels like an observer.

**Use brief solo vignettes.** Between major group scenes, give each player a short solo moment. What is your character doing while the others prepare? The Iron Wall drills with the garrison. The Shadow visits their contacts in the Warrens. The Guest Among Forests walks in the garden and communes with the plants. These vignettes need only be a minute or two each, but they let each character feel like they have a life beyond the party.

**Create parallel tracks with equal weight.** When the party splits, ensure each group has a meaningful task of comparable importance. The group chasing a lead through the archives has the Celestial Book to guide them. The group negotiating with a faction has the Bright Mirror or Guest Among Forests. The group providing backup has the Iron Wall and Iron Bone. Each track is equally important, and each player's skills are essential to their group's success. The groups reconvene to share what they learned, creating natural tradeoffs and hard choices.

**Track screen time between sessions.** After each session, make a quick mental or written note of which players spoke the most and which had the most impact on the outcome. If the same player dominates three sessions in a row, design the next session's situation around another player's backstory or goals. A quiet player is not necessarily an unhappy player, but a session that centers their character will help you learn whether they are engaged or merely patient.

**Use the Twelve Figures as spotlight tools.** When a figure who shares a player's archetype appears, that player naturally gets a moment of recognition. The Night Warbler meeting the Night Warbler NPC. The Celestial Book being summoned by the Celestial Book NPC. These encounters give the player a direct connection to the world and make their archetype feel significant within the larger story. Not every session needs one of these encounters, but every archetype should have at least one such moment per campaign arc.

**The most important rule.** Talk to your players. Ask them individually whether they feel their character has had opportunities to shine. Most players are happy to be patient if they know their moment is coming. A player who trusts that you will deliver their spotlight moment will wait for it with anticipation rather than frustration.

## When Players Surprise You

No plan survives contact with the players. This section covers ten common player actions that can derail a Host's preparation, with practical advice for handling each one. The key principle across all of them is the same: player agency is not a threat to the story. It is the story.

### They Want to Kill a Major NPC

The players have decided that a major figure needs to die. Perhaps the Bright Mirror has become too dangerous. Perhaps the Iron Calculation's schemes have gone too far. Perhaps the Duke of Eternal Night knows too much. Your instinct may be to protect the figure. You have prepared for them. You need them for the story. Resist that instinct.

First, confirm that the players understand the consequences. A public figure cannot be killed without massive repercussions. The investigation will be thorough. The suspects will be many. The players should know what they are walking into. Second, if they proceed, let them try. Make the attempt difficult but possible. A direct assault on one of the Twelve Figures should require planning, resources, and sacrifice. Third, prepare for the aftermath. If they succeed, the power vacuum is enormous. The other eleven figures react. Allies of the dead figure seek revenge. Enemies of the dead figure rush to fill the space. The whole web trembles. If they fail, the consequences are equally dramatic. The figure now knows the players are a threat and will act accordingly.

The key principle: major NPCs are not immortal. They are consequential. Let the players change the world through their actions, even if those actions are violent and destructive. The most memorable campaigns are the ones where the players did something that permanently altered the landscape.

### They Refuse to Engage with the Audit

The players have decided that the Audit is not a challenge to be overcome but a trap to be avoided. They ignore the summons. They flee the city. They go to ground in the Warrens. They refuse to participate in the system that is judging them.

The Audit continues without them. The Commission notes their absence. Their Risk increases automatically. Their Faction Standing drops because they appear guilty. NPCs who depended on them face the Audit alone and may be Sanctioned or Disappeared because the players were not there to provide support. The players cannot escape the consequences by ignoring them. They can only change the form those consequences take.

The key principle: the Court's systems are designed to process everyone. Opting out is itself a choice with consequences. The players may survive the Audit by hiding, but they lose everything they built: their position, their Celestial Favor, their allies. They become fugitives, which is a valid and interesting campaign direction. Let them be fugitives. The game continues.

### They Try to Flee the Capital

The players have decided that the Court is too dangerous. They want to leave the capital entirely and start fresh in a border province or beyond the empire's borders.

Fleeing the capital is possible. The escape itself can be a dramatic session: bribing guards, forging documents, evading the Night Warbler's surveillance network. But the Court does not forget. If the players flee, they become wanted fugitives. Their allies are interrogated. Their property is seized. Their names are added to the Commission's permanent watch list. The Crimson Dusk is everywhere. There is nowhere to go that the Dusk has not touched or will not touch soon. The players may leave the capital, but they cannot leave the empire's dying. The campaign shifts from Court intrigue to survival on the road, with the Dusk always visible on the horizon.

The key principle: the world is bigger than the capital, but the Dusk is bigger than the world. Wherever the players go, the central questions remain: what caused the Dusk, and what can be done about it? The players can change their location. They cannot outrun the premise.

### They Want to Expose the Whole System

The players have gathered enough evidence to prove that the forged edicts exist, the Emperor is imprisoned, and the Grand Council is built on a lie. They want to publish this information to the entire Court.

This is a dramatic and risky play. The publication itself is a challenge: how do you spread information when the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative controls all official channels? The players will need to use rumors, banned texts, public speeches, or infiltrating the official publishing system. Any of these approaches can work. When the truth comes out, the Court does not collapse. It fractures. Some factions believe the players and rally to them. Others denounce them as heretics. The Commission moves to arrest them. The Bright Mirror is forced to take a public position. The Duke of Eternal Night may finally speak openly. The campaign shifts into open conflict. The players are no longer operating in the shadows. They are leading a movement.

The key principle: the truth is powerful but not automatically victorious. Exposing the system does not destroy it. It transforms the conflict from covert to overt. The players must be ready to lead the revolution they have started.

### They Ally With a School You Have Not Developed

The players have decided to throw their lot in with a school or faction that you have not detailed. Perhaps they have chosen the merchants' guild, a minor cult, or a school you mentioned once in passing.

Do not panic. The school they chose has one of the six core needs: preservation, reform, efficiency, continuity, justice, or purity. Assign them the closest match and build from there. Give the school a leader, a want, a fear, and a secret. That is all you need for a session. The school's connections to the existing web will emerge naturally. The players have given you a gift: they have told you what they find interesting. Follow that thread.

The key principle: the Court is vast. You cannot detail all of it. When the players choose an undetailed faction, you build it in response to their interest. The faction becomes whatever the players need it to be, shaped by their interactions. This is faster and more organic than preparing it in advance.

### They Split the Party Across the City

Half the players want to investigate the Archives. The other half want to attend a Council session. The third player wants to follow a suspicious courier through the Outer Market.

Splitting the party is not a problem. It is an opportunity. Each group gets their own scene, and each scene creates information that the other group does not have. The tension comes from the knowledge gap: the Archive group discovers that the Council session is a trap, but they cannot warn the Council group in time. Use the technique from the Adjudication chapter: bounce between groups in short bursts, resolving three to four rounds of action at a time. Keep each burst to five minutes or less. End each burst on a cliffhanger. "You open the document and read the first line. You see a name you recognize. We will come back to this. Let us check on the Council group."

The key principle: a split party creates dramatic irony. The audience (you and the players) knows that the pieces are moving toward collision. The tension is not in what happens next. It is in whether the pieces will arrive in time.

### They Find a Loophole in the Rules

A player has discovered a combination of rules that lets them do something you did not anticipate. Perhaps they have found a way to generate unlimited Celestial Favor, bypass a core mechanic, or invalidate an entire challenge.

First, determine whether the loophole is actually fun. Does it create interesting choices or does it remove all challenge? If it creates interesting choices, let it stand. The player will feel brilliant, and they deserve that feeling. If it removes all challenge, talk to the player privately. "You have found something I did not anticipate. It breaks the game in a way that will make it less fun for everyone. Can we work together to adjust it?" Most players will agree to a fair compromise. If they do not, you have the authority to rule that the loophole does not work. State your reasoning clearly. "This combination produces a result that contradicts the spirit of the rules. I am ruling that it does not function as described."

The key principle: the rules are a framework, not a prison. The Host's authority exists to preserve the game's integrity, not to enforce every technical reading. A player who finds a clever exploit should be rewarded for their cleverness, but not at the cost of the table's experience.

### They Outsmart Your Prepared Mystery Too Quickly

The players have solved in ten minutes a mystery you expected to take three sessions. They asked the right question, connected the right clues, and arrived at the correct conclusion far ahead of schedule.

Congratulate them. They earned it. Then ask yourself: is the mystery truly over, or have they only solved the first layer? The document they found proves the edicts are forged. But who forged them? And who gave the order? And who has been protecting the forger for decades? Each answer leads to a deeper question. The players' quick solution gives you a gift: they have shown you what they are good at. The next mystery can be designed to challenge them at a higher level. For the current session, let them enjoy their victory. Reveal the information they earned and watch their faces light up. Then, in the same session, have the consequences of their discovery arrive. The Commission learns what they know. The forger sends agents to silence them. The truth they uncovered is not the truth they thought it was.

The key principle: when players are clever, reward them with the next challenge, not with a reset. The mystery they solved was always going to lead to deeper waters. They have simply reached the shore faster than expected. The deeper water is still waiting.

### They Want to Use a Dusk Entity as a Weapon

The players have encountered a Dusk Touched being that can be controlled, commanded, or bargained with. They want to use it against their enemies. Perhaps they want to unleash it in the Grand Council Chamber. Perhaps they want to point it at a rival faction's headquarters.

This is a dangerous path. Using the Dusk as a weapon is like using a flood as a garden hose. The entity will do what the players want, but it will also do what it wants. The Dusk is not a tool. It is a corruption of reality that spreads without regard for intentions.

First, determine what the entity wants. The Dusk Touched being that can be controlled is not truly controlled. It is cooperating because it sees an advantage. What does it gain from this arrangement? Second, let the players have their moment of power. The entity does what they ask. It terrifies their enemies. It feels like a victory. Then show them the cost. The entity spreads to things they did not intend to harm. The contamination lingers. The Commission traces the Dusk signature back to them. The entity's demands escalate. What started as a weapon becomes a leash around the players' own necks.

The key principle: the Dusk is not a resource. It is the central problem of the setting. Using it as a shortcut creates consequences that are proportional to the shortcut. The players can weaponize the Dusk, but they cannot weaponize it without being changed by it. Every time they use it, they become a little more like the thing they are trying to stop.

### They Refuse to Trust Any NPC

The players have been burned by betrayals, deceptions, and double crosses. They have decided that no NPC can be trusted. They treat every offer as a trap, every ally as an enemy in waiting, every piece of information as a lie.

This is a natural response to the Court's atmosphere. It can also grind the game to a halt if no relationship can advance.

First, make trust a mechanical choice with clear stakes. "You can accept this official's help without verifying them, which saves you three hours of investigation time. Or you can spend the time confirming they are genuine, which burns daylight but gives you certainty." The players should feel the cost of suspicion in concrete terms, not just in atmosphere. Second, plant one NPC who is exactly what they appear to be. A clerk who is genuinely helpful with no agenda. A guard who gives honest directions. A merchant who charges a fair price. These small honesty signals rebuild trust slowly. Third, when the players do choose to trust someone, honor that trust. If an NPC betrays them, let the betrayal be meaningful and foreshadowed, not arbitrary. The players should feel that their suspicion was a reasonable response to a dangerous world, not a necessary response to a world where everyone is out to get them.

The key principle: paranoia is a survival strategy in the Court, but it is also a prison. The players cannot achieve their goals without forming relationships. Your job is not to trick them into trusting the wrong person. It is to make the act of trust feel like a meaningful choice with real stakes, not a trap.
