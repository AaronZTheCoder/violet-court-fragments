# The Violet Court Fragments

## The Seventh Niche

> _The Court does not publish a change of course._
> _It publishes a change of ancestor._

> _Attributed to the Celestial Book, in a marginal note she denies writing_

---

## The Image

Begin your session with this and describe nothing else until they ask.

The Hall of Open Hearing, on the morning of the twenty second day of Hinge Gate, Year 452. This is the room where the Sun Emperor last spoke aloud to His Court, one hundred and sixty five years ago, on a dispute about the color of a ceremonial thread. Nobody has ruled anything in it since. It is opened perhaps twice in a generation.

Behind the rostrum hangs a portrait four times the height of a man. It is the Ledger Hand, painted from the official likeness of his fortieth year, the one where the mouth is doing something that is not quite a smile and has been argued about by three generations of copyists.

Beneath the portrait stands the Iron Brush, reading an address.

And above him, along the front of the upper gallery, hangs the banner. It is the standing formula, the one that appears on every prefectural wall in the Nine Domains, and it reads: _the Nine Domains, close gathered about the Court and its core, the Steward whose brush is iron._

That is the whole plot in one frame. The dead man's face below. The living man's name above. Let your players see it and let them work out what it means on their own time, because every faction in the empire is working it out this week, and most of them are wrong.

---

## What a Niche Is

The Temple of Ten Thousand Gods stands in the Garden Quarter and it predates the empire. Before the Sun Emperor established the state orthodoxy this was the continent's principal pilgrimage. Ten thousand niches, ten thousand statues, gods brought in by refugees and merchants and conquered peoples across a span of time nobody has successfully measured.

Six of the ten thousand are not gods. Six hold men.

This is the fact that your players will have heard all their lives without ever once thinking about it, the way a person does not think about the load bearing wall of the house they grew up in. The Court has, six times in four hundred and fifty two years, taken a dead official and placed him among the gods, and the Temple has never once been able to refuse.

### The Grades

The Court's rites for its dead were graded and fixed by a regulation of Year 420, which is a short and extremely boring document that decides who is remembered and how loudly.

- **A reading.** Any officer above the fifth rank. A hall in the relevant ministry, a few hundred colleagues, an officer of doctrine speaks for a quarter hour. It happens constantly. Nobody attends who is not required to.
- **A convocation.** Council rank and above. A larger hall, the ministries send deputies, the address is printed and circulated to prefectural offices where it is filed unread.
- **The Full Rite.** Convened jointly by the Grand Council, the Commission for Celestial Purity, the Garrison Command, the assembled Ministries, and the Temple of Ten Thousand Gods. Held in the Hall of Open Hearing. The Steward speaks personally. And at its conclusion, a niche is opened.

The regulation does not say who qualifies for the Full Rite. It says only that the Full Rite is for those who _began_ something. That word has done an enormous amount of work in thirty two years, because it means the Rite is not a reward for service. It is a claim about where a period of history starts, and periods of history that have a beginning can be said to have an end, and a thing that has ended can be replaced.

### The Six

The six are all of the founding and the first century. Two signed the Charter. One held the Nine Domains together through the Sealing. One is the elder who wrote the First Hundred Years Decree. The other two your players will have to look up, and when they do they will find that both entries in the gazetteer are four lines long and that neither says what the man actually did.

The Duke of Eternal Night attended five of the six rites. He was present in the Hall for each one. He knew four of the six personally and disliked three of them.

If your table has a Duke at it, this is his campaign. He is the only living index to what a niche has ever meant, and he is about to watch a seventh opened for a man he regarded, correctly, as a canal administrator with good timing.

## The Fiftieth Year

The Ledger Hand handed over the tablets in Year 402 and walked out of the Spire District on his own feet. Every Steward since has studied that act. None has repeated it.

Year 452 is the fiftieth year since. That is the occasion. It is not his birth, it is not his death, it is the anniversary of his leaving, and the Court chose that anniversary deliberately, and this is the part your players should sit with until it turns over in their hands.

Because in his eleventh year, the Iron Brush had the assembled Court vote to remove the limit on a Steward's term. The measure passed with two abstentions and one vote against, out of nearly three thousand. The Bureau of Harmonious Narrative explained that the change reflected the people's ardent wish for continuity in a period of external turbulence.

So the Court is holding the highest rite of the state to honor a man whose single institutional achievement was leaving on time, and it is holding it fourteen years after abolishing the rule that he leave on time, and it is holding it in a room where nobody has been permitted to disagree out loud in one hundred and sixty five years.

Nobody in the Hall will mention this. Every person in the Hall will have thought of it.

## The Twelve Nights

For six consecutive evenings beginning the eleventh day of Hinge Gate, the lantern relays carried a recitation cycle in twelve parts to every prefectural shrine in the Nine Domains, two parts a night, at the hour when the day's work is done and the households are together.

It was produced jointly by the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative, the Institute of Court History, and the relay authority. It is, by an enormous margin, the largest single act of recitation the empire has ever attempted, and it is very good. That matters. It is not clumsy, it is not shrill, and a Host who plays it as obvious propaganda has thrown away the entire scenario. It is beautifully made and much of it is true.

Alongside the cycle: a set of commemorative seals struck in the Ledger Hand's likeness; a symposium of the Bureau on the Widening and its lasting harmonies; and the designation of the house in the river prefectures where he was born as a place of instruction for the young.

The chief consultant to the cycle is **Dry Ink**, who is past ninety and has not been seen in public in twenty years. He was the Ledger Hand's own draftsman. He wrote the Widening. He physically composed the four pages that let the merchants into the Court, and he has been living quietly in a courtyard in the Academic Quarter for two decades, and somebody went and got him.

The presence of Dry Ink is the single loudest thing about the entire production and almost nobody has noticed it. When you bring back the man who wrote the doctrine to narrate the man who signed it, you are not commemorating a person. You are re issuing a document.

### The Sealed Testimonies

Here is the detail that should make your table sit forward.

The testimonies recited across the twelve nights were not taken this year. They were taken in Years 434 to 436, under the Accord, sixteen to eighteen years ago. They were taken properly, at length, from ministers, from the officiant at his wedding, from the man who sponsored his entry into the Court, from the surviving members of his own household. Then they were sealed and shelved and never issued.

Most of the people who spoke are dead. Several died in the intervening years without ever knowing the recitation existed. One of them, the First Minister **Sharp Frost**, who did the actual work of the trade compact and who had refused every previous request in his life, gave his only testimony to this cycle and died on the morning the fifth night was carried.

So: somebody prepared this rite eighteen years ago and did not perform it.

For what occasion? Nobody now living can say. The requisition that commissioned the testimonies is in the Bureau's own file and it carries no requesting seal.

Your players who have read the Sixth Reconstruction draft will recognize that detail immediately, because the Sixth Reconstruction also arrived in the drafting section without a requesting seal, under the Gloss, and the drafting section has assumed throughout that it came from the Steward's household on no evidence except the assumption.

Two unsigned instructions, eighteen years apart, both maturing in the same thirty days. Do not answer this for your table. Let it sit.

## The Three Narratives

The cycle constructs one man out of three claims, and it never states any of them, which is why it works.

### The First Night: The Uniform

As a young man in the river prefectures, before the Court knew his name, he carried couriers out of a district that had been closed by an authority the Court has since determined had no standing. He did it wearing that authority's own uniform, driving that authority's own cart, past that authority's own checkpoints, for eleven nights.

Two men who were carried out of that district gave testimony confirming it, at length, with dates.

The first night exists because of an accusation. A scholar named **Plain Ledger** published a signed letter alleging that the Ledger Hand's father had held a salaried post under that same unlawful authority, that the son's service record was invented afterward, and that the whole red thread of his origin was a graft onto the line of an uncle who had genuinely died in the Court's service.

Half of it was true. The father's post is a matter of record and has never been denied, and the adoption into the uncle's line is in the official biography. The invention of the service record is not supported by anything.

Plain Ledger was sentenced to ten years for incitement to overturn the Court's determinations. His letter has outlived his sentence and is still copied in the Academic Quarter.

The first night is ninety minutes of testimony in answer to a letter the Court has spent forty years insisting nobody reads.

### The Fifth Night: The Compact

Twenty five rounds of negotiation with the maritime powers over the empire's entry into the trade compact. Six days and six nights without adjournment. On the third night the delegation reported at the first hour of the morning and the Ledger Hand was already awake and already calling the Council into session, and thirteen ministers assembled at the second hour and sat until the fourth. On the fifth day the talks broke and the foreign delegation began packing its cases, and an instruction went out over his own seal that kept them in the room.

The fifth night quotes him: _we will trade with anyone, and we will kneel to no one, and those are not in tension unless you are a fool._

What the fifth night does not contain is any mention of what the empire gave up to sign. The concessions were substantial. They are in the compact, which is public, which almost nobody has read.

And the fifth night does something quieter and more deliberate. Every instruction, every decisive intervention, every sleepless hour is the Ledger Hand's. Sharp Frost, who conducted twenty five rounds personally and whose reputation rests on them, is present throughout the fifth night as a competent and loyal second.

The empire noticed. The empire noticed the same evening.

### The Seventh Night: The Walk

In his fortieth year the Quiet Company walked past the Court compound. They came through the eastern gate at dawn in single file, one at a time, at intervals of perhaps four seconds, walked the length of the compound wall, and went out the western gate, and did this without pause until dusk. They did not stop, chant, or carry banners.

Nobody counted them. That was the problem.

The seventh night is the first time in the history of the Court that the deciding of a proscription has been recited to the public. It shows him unable to sleep. It shows him at a window at the third hour. It shows him asking the same question of four different officers and receiving four different numbers. It shows him deciding.

Previous recitations gave only outcomes. This one gives a process, and the process it gives is a man alone in a room, afraid, choosing.

Grandmother Stone Voice is past ninety and still in the high forest above Nine Willow Bend and remembers the Second Verdant Path Suppression as a personal event rather than a historical one. If your players ever tell her what the seventh night showed, decide in advance how she takes it. There is no obvious answer, and whatever you choose, she will not raise her voice.

## The Ninth Night

Everything above is scene setting. This is the plot.

The Court's practice on the Year of the Long Petition, for ninety six years, has been silence. Not denial. Silence. There is no file. There is a gap in the ledger of the Bureau of Harmonious Records exactly six weeks wide and the paper stock changes after it. Ask an official and you will see a genuine blankness, the expression of a person who has successfully not known something for an entire career.

On the ninth night, the Bureau of Harmonious Narrative issued a statement.

It is four sentences long. It says that in the spring and summer of that year a serious disturbance of good order arose; that at a moment of exceptional delicacy a coastal journal ran a full page attacking the Court's principle of unified deliberation; that the Ledger Hand, then a provincial administrator, moved against the journal decisively and at personal risk; and that his conduct drew the attention of the whole empire.

Every one of those four sentences is defensible. What they omit is the shape of the thing. The journal had printed a mourning notice for a dead reform minded elder, with a passage against government by the aged. He ordered the passage cut. The editor refused. He sent his own propriety officer to seal the presses and the editor was removed, and within a week several hundred thousand people were in the streets of his own prefecture. He went to the capital in a state your players' older characters may remember hearing about, and the First Chair told him only that he had made a simple problem complicated. He returned home and convened the lettered men of his prefecture and criticized himself in front of them, and used the word coward, about himself, out loud.

The ninth night contains none of that. It contains the word decisively.

**And then the ninth night says the thing nobody expected it to say.**

It states, plainly, as a matter of record, that on the day the Court's order went out to the Great Approach, a meeting of certain senior members of the Grand Council was convened, that the Ledger Hand was proposed as Steward, and that the proposal was agreed unanimously.

Sit with the date.

The order going out to the Approach and the naming of the next Steward are the same day. The Approach was still full. The people on it did not know. The Council did not ratify the appointment for another thirty five days, and during those thirty five days the Nine Domains had a First Chair who held the office and a Steward who held the power, and they were not the same man, and the empire was not told.

That is black room governance, admitted in the Bureau's own voice, on the record, to every prefectural shrine in the empire, at the hour when the households are together.

**Who is not in the ninth night.** The First Chair of that year was **Still Reed**. Nine days before the order went out he walked onto the Great Approach before dawn, without escort, and told the people sitting there that he had come too late. He was relieved of every office at the Council's session thirty five days afterward. He lived another sixteen years in a courtyard in the capital with the door watched, and the gardener who tended that courtyard is alive and is seventy one and sells cuttings in the Outer Market on the second and sixth days.

Still Reed is not named on the ninth night. Not once. Not as an opponent, not as a predecessor, not as an absence. The ninth night describes a Stewardship changing hands and contains no previous holder.

**And the images.** When the ninth night reaches the Approach, the recitation shows the Great Approach at dawn. Empty. Swept. The stone is very beautiful in that light.

In Year 423 the Court staged a commemorative cycle for one of the six, and that cycle, when it reached the same six weeks, showed the Approach as it had been, with the people on it, sitting, with their bedding and their copied poetry.

What the empire was permitted to see twenty nine years ago it is not permitted to see now. Nothing was announced. The Bureau simply has a different Key.

**And the address.** In the Hall, the Iron Brush said that the Ledger Hand upheld the Court's correct determinations and preserved the good order of his prefecture.

Read that again. The determination is correct. It was correct then and it is correct now and not one word of it is under revision. The only thing being revised is the man. He has been promoted, posthumously, from a compromise candidate who was lucky in his timing to a pillar raised deliberately in a moment of danger.

The Court has not reopened the question. The Court has upgraded the beneficiary.

## The Three Reckonings

Why is the Iron Brush doing this? He dismantled this man's faction. He reversed this man's two most valuable legacies. He is not sentimental and he does not waste a rite.

**The first reckoning is the debt.** He owes the tablets to the Ledger Hand's people. When the Accord's preferred successor was blocked, it was the Ledger Hand's circle who put forward this quiet provincial administrator with no visible faction and an agreeable manner, and it was the Ledger Hand who settled it. Every person who arranged that has since been broken, retired, or made harmless. The Ledger Hand's own son gave up his post in Year 447 and has held nothing since. The Accord's son holds a deputy office in a ministry of no consequence.

The saying in the Nine Domains for exactly this is old and your players' characters will know it: _he has cleared the mountain, and he still burns incense at the grave._ The debt is paid in rites, which cost nothing, and never in offices, which cost everything. A benefactor who can no longer obstruct you is the safest kind of ancestor.

**The second reckoning is the portrait.** The Court has never in living memory been less of a family, and it therefore requires, urgently, a portrait of a family. Three Stewardships in one room, one continuous line, one unbroken purpose. The Full Rite is the only instrument the Court possesses that can assert continuity without asserting anything falsifiable.

**The third reckoning is the route.** This is the real one.

The Court does not announce a change of course. There is no document for it. Admission is not a thing the Court's grammar can express, and an admission from a Steward who has made correctness a personal attribute is not merely difficult, it is structurally impossible.

So the Court signals with ancestors. When it wants the empire hard, it commemorates the founders. When it wants the empire open, it commemorates the man who opened it. The choice of ancestor is the policy document.

And the empire needs a signal. The merchant houses are sitting on their coin. The great trading families have stopped building. The maritime compacts are being renegotiated by counterparties who have learned to wait. The Court's own review acknowledges insufficient demand, and the granaries are full while the market streets are quiet, and everybody who can read a ledger can see that the last decade of the Clearing of the Source was extremely good at removing rot and has been much less good at making anything grow.

Twelve nights of ports, compacts, foreign delegations, and the Widening, addressed to every prefecture in the empire, and no red sealed decree anywhere. That is the announcement.

And listen to the end of the Iron Brush's address, because that is where the instrument shows. Having praised the Ledger Hand for eleven paragraphs, he turns and lists four undertakings of his own, and the Widening becomes their prologue. The dead man's flag has been carefully cut down and sewn onto the living man's pole.

## The Gap

The three reckonings are sound. They are also going to fail, and the reason is one the Bureau has no instrument for.

A golden statue is made for the living to look at. What the living look at is not the gold. It is the distance between the gold and the floor they are standing on.

Every night of the cycle, the empire has been shown a Nine Domains where a Steward sat down with a chronicler from the maritime powers and was asked to his face whether he ruled alone, and answered the question, in the chronicler's own language, without flinching and without having the chronicler detained. No Steward since has agreed to sit for such a question. The cycle shows this because it is flattering. It does not seem to have occurred to anyone that it is also a measurement.

The empire has been shown an era when a man could be mocked in a teahouse for his singing voice and his fondness for foreign poetry and nobody was taken for it. The cycle shows this as warmth. Your players' characters will hear it as an inventory of things that no longer exist.

Two days after the fifth night, a memorial notice for Sharp Frost circulated in the Academic Quarter and then along the relays. It was not political. It was a paragraph about what it had been like to be twenty five in those years, and it ended by saying that people then had light in their eyes and believed tomorrow would be a better day than today. It was struck from the relays within a day and a half. It is still being copied by hand.

You cannot strike nostalgia. Nostalgia has no author, makes no claim, and violates no article. It is not a false weaving because it is not a weaving. A seal holder cannot be made liable for it because nobody said anything.

This is the first time in eighty one years that the Bureau's Near View has produced a Far View by accident. They gave the empire a face, exactly as the doctrine instructs, and the face turned out to be a comparison.

## Where This Meets the Reconstruction

Run these two books as one campaign. They are the same thirty days and they are the same instrument.

On the seventeenth day of Hinge Gate a slope came down above Gate's End and the count is not known and will not become known. On the nineteenth, a foreign contest house kept a silence and the Bureau removed one word from the notice and handed the empire a quarrel. On the twenty second, the Hall of Open Hearing opened for the first time in a generation and the empire wept for a Steward who has been dead for years.

Two Near Views in six days. The empire has never been so fully occupied.

Nobody is counting Gate's End. That is not a coincidence and it is also not a conspiracy, and your table must eventually understand the difference, because the difference is the whole setting. No one scheduled a mountain. The Full Rite was fixed in the calendar years ago. What happened is that an instrument built to absorb attention absorbed it, on schedule, while a prefecture went uncounted, and every person who made that happen was doing their assigned work correctly.

And under the Sixth Reconstruction, which takes effect at the Hinge, the Twelve Nights stop being an event and become a template. Its second clause moves the Concord Vigil from the ninth day to the third and rewrites it from mourning into thanksgiving. The Twelve Nights are the proof that this can be done at imperial scale and be beautiful. The drafting section cites the cycle. Your players can find the citation.

## The Consecration

The rite is not finished. The address has been read. The niche has not been opened.

The opening of a niche is not a Court act. It cannot be. The Temple of Ten Thousand Gods predates the empire, its authority does not derive from the Court, and that is precisely why a niche there is worth having. A statue the Court installs by its own authority is furniture. A statue the Temple consecrates is something else.

The consecration requires an officiant of the Temple to speak the admission over the opened niche. There are nine officiants. Eight of them are old. The ninth is **Incense Crown**, who is thirty four, the youngest by more than forty years, and who holds the rite on which the entire legality of the Celestial Court rests.

The Court has asked. The Court has been extremely courteous about it. The Court has not yet been refused, because she has not yet answered.

If she consecrates, the Temple that stood before the empire becomes, in that moment and forever after, an office of it. Every one of the ten thousand niches is retroactively the Court's to allocate.

If she refuses, then the woman who performs the rite that makes the Court lawful has publicly declined to perform a rite for the Court, and there is no version of the following month in which she survives that, and there is no version in which the empire fails to notice what her refusal implies about everything else she has ever consecrated.

**And the date.** The consecration is set for the third intercalary day of the Hinge.

The Key turns on the first.

Every instruction governing the consecration, including the Temple's own instructions, including whatever Incense Crown is told to say, was drafted in the current register and will be executed in the next one. If the two registers do not agree on a single term in the admission formula, the rite performed will not be the rite commissioned, and under the Court's own doctrine a rite performed wrongly is not a rite that failed. It is a rite that did something else.

The eighth phrase of her rite has a rising tone whose correctness has been disputed for one hundred and forty years. She is the only living person who has ever performed it. Nobody, including her, knows what it does.

## Running the Seventh Niche

**What the players are.** They are Eighth Rank copyists in the Bureau of Harmonious Records, which means the ninth night's statement passed through a room like theirs before it went out, and the requisition for the sealed testimonies is filed somewhere they can reach, and the Temple's correspondence about the consecration is being copied by somebody at their rank this week.

**Do not make the cycle stupid.** The single fastest way to lose this scenario is to let the table dismiss the Twelve Nights as crude. Play a whole recitation straight. Let a player's own character be moved by it. Let the courage be real, because it was. The horror is not that the empire is being told lies. The horror is that it is being told selected truths by people who believe them, in a beautiful voice, on schedule.

**Scenes that work.**

- A player is handed the ninth night's four sentences to copy fair, and has forty minutes and no authority, and notices the date.
- The gardener in the Outer Market, seventy one, who tended a watched courtyard for sixteen years and sells cuttings on the second and sixth days and has never told anyone anything.
- Dry Ink, past ninety, in a courtyard in the Academic Quarter, who wrote the Widening and will talk about the paper it was drafted on for an hour before he says one useful thing.
- The Duke of Eternal Night in the Hall, watching a seventh niche opened, saying nothing whatsoever, which the players will find far more disturbing than anything he could say.
- Incense Crown asking a player character, directly, what she should do. She is thirty four. She has nobody.

**The schools.** Golden Orthodoxy is appalled by the Temple's involvement and cannot say so. Iron Calculation reads the third reckoning correctly within a day and begins repositioning. Crimson Lineage remembers all six previous rites and is the only faction that can say what they cost. Common Flame is watching the empire fall in love with an era of merchants and is losing an argument it did not know it had entered. Verdant Path has the seventh night to contend with and is split down the middle by it. Bright Mirror considers the entire proceeding a doctrinal irregularity and is, on the narrow legal point, correct.

**What the table must not be told too early.** That the requisitions match. That the consecration falls after the Key turns. That nobody scheduled the mountain. Especially that last one.

## Threads

- The requisition for the sealed testimonies carries no seal. Neither does the Sixth Reconstruction draft. The two documents are eighteen years apart and use the same hand for the file notation.
- Still Reed's gardener is alive, is seventy one, and sells cuttings in the Outer Market on the second and sixth days of the month.
- The Year 423 cycle exists in the relay archive with its images intact, including the Approach with the people on it. It has not been withdrawn. Nobody has thought to withdraw it.
- Plain Ledger completed his ten years. Somebody is still copying his letter, and the copies are recent, and the hand is educated.
- Dry Ink was brought back by someone. He did not volunteer, and he will not say who asked.
- Sharp Frost refused every request of his life and gave this one testimony. Something changed his mind in Year 435, and the man who carried the request to him is still in the Bureau.
- Incense Crown must answer before the third intercalary day, and the Key turns on the first, and she has not been told that the Key turns at all, because she is not of the Court and nobody thought to mention it.
